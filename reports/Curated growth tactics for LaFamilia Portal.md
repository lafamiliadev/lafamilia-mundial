# Repair the reply loop, then recruit

LaFamilia Portal does not have an acquisition problem; it has a dead interaction loop, and every framework, case and field experiment reviewed says the loop must be fixed before growth is pushed. The portal holds **1,915 vetted accounts, 100 investors opted into deal sourcing and 51 founders actively raising (median round $1M)**, yet in 71 days since the 13 September launch only **7 members sent 44 intro requests, 4 received a reply (9%), and none were sent in the last 30 days**, while 79 new members activated in that same window and did nothing afterward. The evidence base is unambiguous about what this means: a first request that goes unanswered is the dominant churn point in online communities ([Joyce & Kraut 2006](https://dx.doi.org/10.1145%2F1124772.1124916)) and in two-sided marketplaces, where rejected or ignored requesters leave the platform ([Fradkin, Airbnb](https://ide.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/SearchMatchingEfficiency.pdf?x41178)), and reactivating dormant members is four times harder than getting active ones to do more ([Shmukler, LinkedIn](https://developernation.net/blog/growth-lessons-from-linkedin)). Every curated network that grew without diluting (AngelList 2010, YC, Endeavor, Hampton, Latitud) manufactured its first transactions by hand inside a narrow slice before automating, and the one mechanic present in every retention-positive case and absent in every collapse is a small, fixed, facilitated peer group ([Kraut & Resnick](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/kraut10-Newcomers-current.pdf); [TNW on AngelList](https://thenextweb.com/news/the-quiet-rise-of-angellist); [EO Forum](https://www.eonetwork.org/getstarted)). The recommended 90-day plan is therefore: hand-broker double-opt-in intros between the 51 raising founders and matched investors, make investor capacity and responsiveness visible with a Handshake-style yes/no/redirect and a 7-day clock, guarantee every first request a reply, reactivate dormant members only through active members and true social proof, and grow membership through nominations from engaged members (investors first) with low single-digit quotas and a published vetting pass rate as the guardrail. North-star metrics should be 7-day reply rate and meetings per week, not account counts; with 252 activated members only very large effects are detectable, so most experiments below are pre-registered before/after designs with holdouts, and the two randomized tests run on the full 1,915-account funnel.

## 1. The constraint is a 9% reply rate, not a 13% activation rate

The portal's 13% activation (252 of 1,915) looks like the headline problem, but the funnel breaks later and harder. Among 252 activated members, **7 (2.8%) have ever requested an intro**, and of the 51 founders raising, **only 19 (37%) have published to deal flow**, against which investors have made **4 deal-flow requests (0.2 per published raise)**. The 61 members who earned the profile-completion reward converted to 4 redemptions, and 7 live perks have been touched by 3 members. The supply side is not the problem in the usual marketplace sense: **100 of 116 logged-in investors (86%) opted into deal sourcing, 66 offer mentoring and 40 offer intros**. Both sides are present and willing on paper, and almost no transactions occur. Casey Winters names this exact state: the early metrics that matter are "conversion rate between supply and demand, enough transactions that supply sticks around, and enough quality that demand comes back," measured per local market rather than in company-wide totals that "conceal markets that do not work" ([First Round Review, Winters](https://review.firstround.com/how-to-build-a-scalable-marketplace-dos-and-donts-from-the-growth-expert-behind-grubhub-pinterest-and-more/)). Sarah Tavel's framing is the same: Level 1 is a "constrained opportunity" where the job is user happiness in a small market, and "GMV is a vanity metric. Happy GMV is what matters" ([Tavel](https://sarahtavel.medium.com/the-hierarchy-of-marketplaces-introduction-and-level-1-983995aa218e)).

The academic record explains why the current numbers are self-reinforcing. In Joyce and Kraut's study of 2,777 Usenet newcomers, those whose first post received a reply were substantially more likely to post again ([Joyce & Kraut 2006](https://dx.doi.org/10.1145%2F1124772.1124916)); Kraut and Resnick's design claims distilled from this and related work are that asking specific, well-matched people to do specific tasks beats broadcasting, that people comply more when they see others have complied, and that newcomers whose first contribution is ignored or rejected leave ([Kraut & Resnick, contribution chapter](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Kraut10-Contribution-current.pdf); [Halfaker et al., Wikipedia](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Research:The_Rise_and_Decline)). On Airbnb, roughly **49% of inquiries were rejected or ignored, only 15% led to a transaction, and rejected searchers were more likely to leave the platform** ([Fradkin](https://ide.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/SearchMatchingEfficiency.pdf?x41178)). Two-thirds of new Facebook members never posted in their first two weeks, and seeing friends contribute was what predicted later sharing ([Burke, Marlow & Lento 2009](https://research.fb.com/publications/feed-me-motivating-newcomer-contribution-in-social-network-sites/)). The portal's 9% reply rate is below even the vendor-reported **20–25% median investor opt-in to intro requests** on dedicated fundraising platforms ([Metal.so](https://www.metal.so/data-desk/intro-response-benchmark)), which is itself a low bar for a vetted, identity-visible community.

Chen's framework locates the "hard side" of a network as the minority who do disproportionate work and are hard to attract and keep; for marketplaces it is usually supply ([Chen, Tinder chapter](https://andrewchen.com/solve-a-hard-problem-cold-start-problem/); [a16z book page](https://a16z.com/books/the-cold-start-problem/)). Here the hard side is investor attention, and the portal has already collected it: 100 opted-in investors is an asset most founder communities never assemble. What is missing is Chen's atomic network, "the smallest network needed with enough density and stability to stand on its own" ([a16z](https://a16z.com/books/the-cold-start-problem/)). Every documented seed was socially or geographically bounded: Tinder's ~500 attendees at one USC party, Uber's city-by-city launches, Clubhouse's "Paul and Paul's friends in the San Francisco Bay Area," and LinkedIn's 13 insiders inviting 112 people on launch day ([Chen](https://andrewchen.com/solve-a-hard-problem-cold-start-problem/); [Built In, Chen excerpt](https://builtin.com/articles/andrew-chen-cold-start-problem); [TechCrunch, Chen on Clubhouse](https://techcrunch.com/2021/12/08/andrew-chen-of-a16z-on-how-startups-get-past-a-cold-start-to-survive-and-thrive); [CNN Money, Hoffman](https://money.cnn.com/2009/06/02/smallbusiness/linkedin_startup_story.smb/)). With **152 of 252 activated members in the US and a median $1M round**, the natural atomic network is US seed-stage founders and the investors who write that cheque, not 86 cities in 19 countries at once.

## 2. Every network that stayed curated brokered its first intros by hand

The closest analogue to the portal's current state is AngelList in 2010. It began as "a curated email distribution list of angel and seed stage investors," with Nivi and Naval screening both the deals pushed to the list and the investors allowed to receive them, starting from roughly 25–50 angels and a weekly digest of three pitches ([TNW, Oct 2010](https://thenextweb.com/news/the-quiet-rise-of-angellist); [TechCrunch, 2010](https://techcrunch.com/?p=490380)). Investors pulled: by July 2011 an investor had asked for an intro to a startup more than 8,000 times, and the 2011 yearbook recorded **500 startups, 2,500 investors and 12,500 introductions** ([Venture Hacks](https://venturehacks.com/centi-sesquicentennial); [TechCrunch, Jan 2012](https://techcrunch.com/2012/01/29/angellist-launches-2011-yearbook-500-startups-2500-investors-12500-introductions/)). Ravikant's own account is that they "always did things manually until they understood how the process worked," then automated ([Vator](https://vator.tv/n/16cd)), and publishing aggregate activity (219 funded, 8,000 intros) was itself the social proof that pulled in new investors while screening was still manual ([Venture Hacks, Active AngelList](https://venturehacks.com/active-angellist)). Paul Graham generalises the lesson as "do things that don't scale," with Stripe's founders setting users up on their own laptops rather than sending a link ([Graham](https://paulgraham.com/ds.HTML)).

YC's investor-side machinery is the most specific template for making investors respond. Demo Day invitations are "software generated and based on recent investment history in YC startups," and in-person seats are reserved for investors who put at least $50,000 into YC companies in the prior two years ([YC Demo Day FAQ](https://www.ycombinator.com/demoday/faq/); [TechCrunch, Sept 2024](https://techcrunch.com/2024/09/25/y-combinators-next-demo-day-will-include-in-person-seats-for-top-vcs-garry-tan-says/)). The Handshake Deal Protocol requires a single-amount, unconditional offer with a deadline so an investor cannot delay indefinitely, and a paper trail so YC "can track whether investors are breaking their verbal agreements" ([YC Handshake](https://ycombinator.com/handshake)). Investor Day used mutual ranking (investors "liked" companies, both sides ranked each other) and software-generated 20-minute slots so each company met about 15 investors in a day; **over 80% of investors who liked companies had meetings scheduled and more than 97% of founders and investors said they would do it again** ([YC blog, Investor Day software](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/investor-day-software)). Bookface adds one-directional accountability, founder-written reviews of investors that investors cannot answer publicly ([YC software page](https://ycombinator.com/software)). Endeavor makes selection itself the mentor product: six mentors interview each candidate and must vote unanimously, fewer than 7% of screened companies are selected, and "each step doubles as a mentorship opportunity," which is how a volunteer network of 3,500+ mentors stays engaged ([Endeavor US](https://us.endeavor.org/?p=29); [Endeavor](https://endeavor.org/?p=428785); [Endeavor mentors](https://endeavor.org/global-community/mentors/)). Techstars scores mentors through a founder-filled "Mentor Deck" and publishes annual All-Star Mentor lists ([Techstars mentor FAQ](https://www.techstars.com/mentor-faq)).

The Latin American cases confirm the sequence. Latitud built an equity-free fellowship of 700–800 founders first, raised an $11.5M a16z/NFX seed on that traction, then seeded its investor side by status rather than volume with an Angel Fellowship of unicorn founders, and by 2025 narrowed to eight pre-founders per San Francisco cohort from 700+ applications ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=2289987); [a16z](https://a16z.com/announcement/investing-in-latitud/); [Latitud](https://latitud.com/blog/latitud-sf-fellowship-explained)). Platanus Ventures runs a Spanish-language programme with a 0.9% selection rate, a biweekly "Coliseo" peer office hour, hackathons as top of funnel, and a 25-seat angel club, Kernel, with three to four cohorts a year to manufacture its investor side in batches ([La República](https://www.larepublica.co/globoeconomia/estas-son-las-nuevas-startups-chilenas-seleccionadas-por-platanus-ventures-3728877); [Diario Financiero](https://www.df.cl/df-lab/innovacion-y-startups/platanus-ventures-internacionaliza-sus-hackathones-lanza-club-de); [Luma, Coliseo](https://luma.com/rtf8dwpn)).

The paid networks show what retains members once they are in. Hampton reached about 300 members at launch through founder DMs and word of mouth, grew to 1,000+ members and roughly $8M ARR, accepts 2–8% of applicants after founder-run interviews, lets any member object before someone is admitted, and places members in eight-person size-matched core groups with a facilitator ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/90874657/hampton-sam-parr-membership-tech-ceo); [Inc.](https://www.inc.com/cara-cannella/inside-hampton-sam-parrs-bootstrapped-solution-to-the-founder-loneliness-epidemic/91284896); [Hampton FAQ](https://joinhampton.com/faq)). It raised its revenue floor from $1M to $3M eighteen months in, tightening the gate as the brand grew ([Parr on X](https://x.com/thesamparr/status/1829138094064390203)). Chief scaled to 20,000 members on employer-paid dues and an 8–12 person facilitated Core group, and its 2023 backlash centred on members estimating up to 50% churn from what they saw inside those groups and calling them "hit or miss" ([Fortune, Mar 2023](https://fortune.com/2023/03/16/chief-womens-network-startup-price-valuation-waitlist-members/)). EO's forum, the ancestor of both, is 6–10 local peers meeting monthly under a confidentiality norm with no pitching ([EO](https://www.eonetwork.org/getstarted)). Superhuman gated every new user behind a 30-minute one-to-one onboarding call, each specialist closing about 50 customers a week, and used the "very disappointed" survey to move its product-market-fit score from 22% toward Sean Ellis's 40% bar by building only for the segment that already loved it ([TechCrunch, Feb 2020](https://techcrunch.com/2020/02/28/superhuman-ceo-rahul-vohra-on-waitlists-freemium-pricing-and-future-products/); [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit)).

The failures are as instructive. Clubhouse's two-invites-per-user quota produced tenfold monthly growth to 10M+ weekly actives, but downloads had already fallen about 90% (9.6M in February 2021 to 922,000 in April) before invites were removed in July 2021, and Davison later said "we grew way, way too fast" ([9to5Mac, Sensor Tower](https://9to5mac.com/2021/05/03/clubhouse-downloads-plummet-to-900000-in-april-as-competition-grows/); [TNW](https://thenextweb.com/news/clubhouse-opens-to-everyone-ends-invite-only-policy); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2023/04/27/clubhouse-layoffs-app-reached-4-billion-valuation-during-pandemic.html)). Its gate was "knows a member," not fit, so the quota amplified low-intent users. Dribbble's member-issued invites throttled growth for 12 years but created a resale market ($25–$80 per invite) and hoarding; its 2019 fix was to allocate invites to engaged members and let prospects upload work before a member spends an invite, and in 2021 it added an application route ([Intercom podcast](https://www.intercom.com/blog/podcasts/dribbble-ceo-zack-onisko-community/); [Dribbble 2019](https://dribbble.com/stories/2019/08/18/guide-to-inviting-new-designers-to-dribbble); [Dribbble 2021](https://dribbble.com/stories/2021/04/29/a-bigger-more-inclusive-dribbble)). On Deck cloned one fellowship into communities serving 10,000+ people in two years, then cut roughly a quarter and a third of staff in 2022; a former admissions employee wrote that "once we tied employee compensation to revenue and the size of the cohort, the quality bar started to slip" ([TechCrunch, Aug 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/08/04/on-deck-lays-off-a-third-of-staff-after-cutting-a-quarter-just-months-prior); [Boring Business Nerd](https://boringbusinessnerd.com/post/what-i-learned-working-in-a-hypergrowth-startup)). Hoffman's own LinkedIn history includes the warning that the contact-import mechanic that "spiked" growth also left "some people with a spammy impression" ([Fast Company](https://www.pressreader.com/usa/fast-company/20230301/283132843002090)); his "let fires burn" triage asks which fire kills the company first, and for a trust-based investor network that fire is low-quality requests reaching investors, not slow feature delivery ([Hoffman](https://www.reidhoffman.org/why-the-best-entrepreneurs-let-fires-burn/); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2018/10/01/reid-hoffman-blitzscaling-book/)).

## 3. Investors reply to visible capacity, bounded asks and upward-only norms

Field experiments identify which signals move the responding side. On Fiverr, across 11.66 million transactions and eight experiments, **a one-hour reply delay cut hiring odds about 46% and a reply after 24 hours was equivalent to no reply**, independent of ratings, and the advantage vanished when the reply looked automated ([Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2024.06185)). On oDesk, letting workers signal spare capacity led them to receive more invitations, reject fewer and be hired more, with up to a 6% market surplus gain ([Horton 2019](https://john-joseph-horton.com/papers/buyer-uncertainty-about-seller-capacity-causes-consequences-and-a-partial-solution/)), and algorithmic candidate recommendations raised fill rates about 20%, concentrated in openings with thin applicant pools ([Horton 2017](https://john-joseph-horton.com/papers/the-effects-of-algorithmic-labor-market-recommendations-evidence-from-a-field-experiment/)). Doubling the size of a rental marketplace lowered confirmation rates 15.4% and occupancy 15.9% because search is costly and time-boxed ([Li & Netessine](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2018.3223)), which is the empirical case for routing two or three matched investors to a founder rather than a browsable directory of 100. Hagiu and Wright define the platform's value as "enabling direct interactions between at least two distinct types of affiliated customers," and the HBR platform literature warns that poor curation causes "poor quality contributions or misbehavior of some participants" that make others defect ([Hagiu & Wright](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:indorg:v:43:y:2015:i:c:p:162-174); [Van Alstyne, Parker & Choudary](https://courses.edx.org/asset-v1:BUx+QD505x+2T2017+type@asset+block@6_Reasons_Platforms_Fail_-_HBR.org.pdf)).

Defaults and forced choices do most of the work at low cost. Opt-out framing raised stated organ-donor consent from 42% to 82%, and a neutral forced-choice condition reached 79%, not significantly different from opt-out ([Johnson & Goldstein](https://www.dangoldstein.com/papers/JohnsonGoldstein_Defaults_Transplantation2004.pdf)); automatic 401(k) enrollment raised participation from 37% to 86% ([Madrian & Shea](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7682.pdf)). The transfer is a required yes/no at onboarding ("open to N intros this month?") and a one-click reply template (yes / not now / refer to someone else), which captures most of the default gain without a silent default "yes" that requesters would experience as ghosting. The dating-market "one-way mirror" experiment shows that removing the weak signal of a visible profile view cut matches about 14% for the side that rarely initiates ([Bapna et al.](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2015.2301)); "an investor in your sector viewed your raise" is the portal's equivalent, and it costs nothing when true.

Social-proof messaging needs care. Descriptive norms raised hotel towel reuse from about 35% to 44% ([Bohner & Schlüter replication of Goldstein et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4118982)), and Zurich students told that a high share of peers contributed to social funds gave more ([Frey & Meier](https://www.cesifo.org/DocDL/750.pdf)), but the MovieLens experiment is the warning: personalised comparison to the community median raised below-median users' ratings 530% and **cut above-median users' activity 62%** ([Chen, Harper, Konstan & Li 2010](https://mail.yanchen.people.si.umich.edu/papers/soc_pref_movielens_20090619_aer.pdf)). A published "average investor reply rate" would pull the portal's best responders down; comparisons should be upward-only ("you are in the top quartile of responders") or against a high reference group, consistent with Kraut and Resnick's claim to emphasise how many already participate rather than how much the community needs ([Kraut & Resnick, newcomers](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/kraut10-Newcomers-current.pdf)). Reciprocity decays with volume: 20% of strangers returned an unsolicited Christmas card in 1976, 26% when the sender carried a "Dr." title, and only about 2% in a 2015 replication when unsolicited mail read as spam ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/psysociety/i-8217-ll-show-you-my-holiday-card-if-you-show-me-yours/); [Discover](https://www.discovermagazine.com/mind/only-2-of-people-will-return-a-christmas-card-from-a-stranger)). Badges increase participation but steer effort toward whatever earns them ([Anderson et al. 2013](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2013/proceedings/p95.pdf)), so the profile-completion reward (61 earned, 4 redeemed) is currently paying for the wrong behaviour. Scarcity's average effect on value is small (r ≈ 0.12) and reads as a quality signal only when attributed to selectivity rather than artificial supply limits ([Lynn 1991](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstream/handle/1813/71652/Lynn60_Scarcity_s_enhancement.pdf?sequence=1)).

Network structure defines what the portal is selling. Granovetter's job-changers found 56% of jobs through contacts and 84% of those through weak ties ([Granovetter 2005](https://cpi.stanford.edu/_media/pdf/Reference%20Media/Granovetter_2005_Social%20Networks.pdf)); Burt's brokers spanning structural holes were better paid, better evaluated and produced ideas rated more valuable ([Burt 2004](https://snap.stanford.edu/class/cs224w-readings/Burt04StructureHole.pdf)); and LinkedIn's randomised People You May Know experiments on 20 million users confirmed causally that moderately weak ties, a few mutual connections rather than none or many, raise job mobility most ([Rajkumar et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36107999/)). A shared Latino founder-investor identity supplies exactly that moderate tie strength, so the portal's product is brokerage across sub-networks (Mexico City founders to Miami angels, US seed founders to LatAm-focused funds). Uzzi's paradox of embeddedness is the ceiling: strong-tie networks raise survival up to a threshold and then insulate members from outside information ([Uzzi 1997](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/1997/social-structure-and-competition-in-interfirm-networks-the-paradox)), which argues for deliberately seeding bridges to non-Latino investors rather than closing the circle.

## 5. Ten experiments, ranked by expected effect on the reply loop

Sample sizes below follow the standard two-proportion z-test at α = 0.05 and 80% power (the formula behind [Evan Miller's calculator](https://www.evanmiller.org/ab-testing/sample-size.html)), computed in the metrics research note rather than taken from a source: per arm, 10%→20% needs 199, 20%→30% needs 293, 30%→45% needs 162, 5%→10% needs 434 and 13%→20% needs 440; splitting 252 activated members 50/50 detects only 10%→23% or 30%→47%, while splitting all 1,915 accounts (950 per arm) detects 10%→14% or 20%→25%. Kohavi's record is that real lifts are "typically under one percent, rarely over two or three percent," that nine of ten surprising results at Microsoft and Airbnb had an invalidating flaw, and that an underpowered significant result exaggerates the true lift by roughly 4–5x ([Kohavi et al.](https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/research/published/KohaviEtAlMarch2026.pdf); [Kohavi, published A/B critique](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1IDcLEr58QoQU2VJPc-TefLMLegdQMnh3/mobilebasic)). Sequential methods allow early stopping on large effects but "low traffic doesn't disappear with a better method" ([Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/sequential-testing-the-secret-sauce-for-low-volume-a-b-tests-fe62bdf9627b/)), and holdouts below about 5% are meaningless at this scale ([CXL](https://cxl.com/blog/hold-out-groups/)). Consequently only experiments 4 and 8 are true randomised tests; the rest are pre-registered before/after designs with 10–20% holdouts or crossovers, with hypothesis, metric, n and stop date written before launch, and any lift above twice the pre-registered minimum treated as an instrumentation check first. Each experiment also needs the weekly customer interviews Torres prescribes for discovery at this scale ([Product Talk](https://www.producttalk.org/glossary-discovery-continuous-discovery/)).

**1. Hand-brokered double-opt-in intros in one slice.** Hypothesis: operator-selected, two-to-three-investor matches for each raising founder, sent with a substantive endorsement blurb and investor opt-in before the founder is forwarded, lift the 7-day investor reply rate from 9% to at least 40%. Segment: the 51 raising founders, starting with US seed-stage, matched against the 100 deal-sourcing investors by stage, sector and geography. Metric: share of brokered requests answered (accept, decline or redirect) within 7 days; secondary, meetings booked. Sample and design: 120–150 brokered requests over six weeks, compared against the 44-request baseline; a 9→40 point move is detectable well below the 126-per-arm threshold that resolves 10%→23%. Duration: 6 weeks. Kill: fewer than 20% replied after the first 60 requests (below the vendor median), which means matching quality, not channel, is the problem. Evidence: [TNW, AngelList](https://thenextweb.com/news/the-quiet-rise-of-angellist); [Horton 2017](https://john-joseph-horton.com/papers/the-effects-of-algorithmic-labor-market-recommendations-evidence-from-a-field-experiment/); [SaaStr on double opt-in](https://www.saastr.com/dear-saastr-why-are-vcs-so-adamant-about-warm-intros); [Kraut & Resnick](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Kraut10-Contribution-current.pdf).

**2. Investor capacity declaration and one-click reply.** Hypothesis: a forced-choice monthly prompt ("open to 0 / 2 / 5 intros this month") plus a yes / not now / refer button with a 7-day clock raises reply rate at least 15 points over experiment 1's first four weeks and cuts median time to reply below 72 hours. Segment: the 100 deal-sourcing investors; 20 held out and never prompted. Metric: 7-day reply rate and median hours to reply on brokered requests. Sample and design: before/after across the two halves of experiment 1 with the 20-investor holdout; with 50 investors per arm only a 30-point difference is detectable in a randomised design, so this is pre-registered before/after. Duration: 8 weeks. Kill: prompted investors do not out-reply the holdout by 15 points, or more than 30% declare zero capacity (fatigue signal). Evidence: [Horton 2019](https://john-joseph-horton.com/papers/buyer-uncertainty-about-seller-capacity-causes-consequences-and-a-partial-solution/); [Johnson & Goldstein](https://www.dangoldstein.com/papers/JohnsonGoldstein_Defaults_Transplantation2004.pdf); [Fiverr speed](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2024.06185); [YC Handshake](https://ycombinator.com/handshake).

**3. Guaranteed reply to every first request.** Hypothesis: if every founder's first intro request receives a human reply (accept, decline with reason, or redirect) within 7 days, with operators chasing investors on day 4, the share of first-time requesters who send a second request within 30 days exceeds 60%, and their 60-day active rate exceeds that of the pre-launch cohort of 7 senders. Segment: all founders making a first request (51 raising plus 31 planning to raise). Metric: second-request rate within 30 days; 7-day first-request reply rate. Sample and design: before/after on roughly 80 first requests; no holdout, because withholding a reply is the harm being tested. Duration: 10 weeks. Kill: fewer than 70% of first requests can be answered within 7 days even with chasing, which shows investor supply, not founder motivation, is binding and routes effort to experiments 2 and 6. Evidence: [Joyce & Kraut](https://dx.doi.org/10.1145%2F1124772.1124916); [Fradkin](https://ide.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/SearchMatchingEfficiency.pdf?x41178); [Halfaker et al.](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Research:The_Rise_and_Decline).

**4. Dormant reactivation through active members (randomised).** Hypothesis: a personal message from a named active member or operator carrying true, specific social proof ("three investors in fintech joined this month; one asked about companies at your stage") activates dormant vetted accounts at twice the rate of the weekly WhatsApp/LinkedIn digest. Segment: the roughly 1,660 vetted-but-dormant accounts, randomised 50/50 (about 830 per arm), stratified by role and country. Metric: onboarding completion within 14 days; secondary, first action within 30 days. Sample and design: 830 per arm detects a 5%→10% move (434 required). Duration: 4 weeks plus 14-day window. Kill: lift below 3 absolute points, or unsubscribe/complaint rate above 1% in either arm. Evidence: [Shmukler, 5% vs 20% CTR](https://developernation.net/blog/growth-lessons-from-linkedin); [Kraut & Resnick claims 3 and 9](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/kraut10-Newcomers-current.pdf); [Burke et al.](https://research.fb.com/publications/feed-me-motivating-newcomer-contribution-in-social-network-sites/); [Facebook 2018 cautionary case](https://boingboing.net/2018/07/21/poisoning-the-well.html).

**5. Forced-choice deal-flow publishing with "viewed your raise" signal.** Hypothesis: asking every raising founder an explicit publish / not now question with the proof line "100 investors have opted in to see raises," and notifying founders when an investor views their raise, lifts publication from 37% to at least 60% and deal-flow requests from 0.2 to at least 1 per published raise in 30 days. Segment: 51 raising and 31 planning founders, plus new founder activations. Metric: publication rate within 7 days of prompt; deal-flow requests per published raise. Sample and design: before/after on about 80 founders; a 37→60 move is near the 30%→47% boundary detectable at 126 per arm, so pre-register and accept directional evidence. Duration: 6 weeks. Kill: lift under 15 points, or published raises still draw fewer than 0.5 requests each (then investor engagement, not founder publishing, is binding). Evidence: [Johnson & Goldstein](https://www.dangoldstein.com/papers/JohnsonGoldstein_Defaults_Transplantation2004.pdf); [Bapna et al.](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2015.2301); [Kraut & Resnick claim 9](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/kraut10-Newcomers-current.pdf).

**6. Monthly matching day with mutual ranking.** Hypothesis: a virtual Investor Day, where opted-in investors "like" published raises, both sides rank, and software assigns 20-minute slots, produces at least one held meeting per participating founder and schedules at least 70% of investors who liked a company. Segment: published founders (19 now, target 30) and investors who declared capacity in experiment 2. Metric: meetings held per founder; share of liking investors matched; would-repeat rate. Sample and design: two cycles, before/after against the current zero meetings; outcome survey required within 48 hours of each meeting. Duration: 8 weeks. Kill: under 50% of liking investors matched, or would-repeat below 70% on either side. Evidence: [YC Investor Day, 80%/97%](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/investor-day-software); [Li & Netessine](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2018.3223); [Lunchclub feedback loop](https://www.forbes.com/sites/frederickdaso/2019/09/27/lunchclub-an-ai-relationship-curator-startup-raises-4m-from-a16z-to-connect-professionals-offline/).

**7. Stage-matched founder pods.** Hypothesis: founders placed in a fixed 8–10 person, stage-matched, facilitated monthly pod with a confidentiality and no-pitching norm are active in at least 4 of 8 weeks at twice the rate of unpodded founders and send more intro requests. Segment: 134 logged-in founders; 60 placed into six pods, 60 waitlisted as control, matched on stage and revenue status (89 of 134 report revenue; 31 are repeat founders). Metric: active in 4 of 8 weeks; intro requests per founder; "very disappointed" share at week 8. Sample and design: 60 per arm detects only very large effects, so the randomised waitlist is pre-registered with directional thresholds and the Ellis survey as the quality check. Duration: 8 weeks, then control pods start. Kill: pod attendance below 50% by session three, the Chief "hit or miss" failure mode. Evidence: [EO Forum](https://www.eonetwork.org/getstarted); [Hampton core groups](https://www.inc.com/cara-cannella/inside-hampton-sam-parrs-bootstrapped-solution-to-the-founder-loneliness-epidemic/91284896); [Fortune on Chief](https://fortune.com/2023/03/16/chief-womens-network-startup-price-valuation-waitlist-members/); [Kraut & Resnick claim 23, mentorship](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/kraut10-Newcomers-current.pdf); [First Round, Superhuman survey](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit).

**8. Nomination-based growth with investor-weighted quotas (randomised on the inviter side).** Hypothesis: giving engaged members two nominations per quarter, with investors and repeat founders receiving them first, yields nominees whose vetting pass rate and 30-day activation exceed inbound applicants', because selection effects follow the inviter's motivation. Segment: 252 activated members randomised into nomination-enabled (126) and not-yet-enabled (126), with the roughly 72 inbound applicants per quarter as the comparison pool for nominee quality. Metric: nominations used per enabled member; vetting pass rate of nominees vs inbound; 30-day activation of admitted nominees; days to decision. Sample and design: the inviter randomisation only detects whether nominations are used at all (10%→23% boundary); nominee quality is a before/after comparison against inbound. Duration: one quarter. Kill: nominee pass rate below inbound pass rate, or nominees' 30-day activation below inbound, or more than 80% of nominations unused (Dribbble hoarding). Evidence: [Chen on Uber's driver selection effect](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/56962799-the-cold-start-problem/7227827-samuel-van-deth/c87a6bd6-af02-4bb0-97ef-8caf29924674); [Dribbble 2019](https://dribbble.com/stories/2019/08/18/guide-to-inviting-new-designers-to-dribbble); [Hampton member objection](https://joinhampton.com/faq); [Gmail quota history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Gmail); [Koch & Benlian, referral scarcity](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1094996815000481).

**9. Upward-only responsiveness norms in the digest (crossover).** Hypothesis: a digest line reporting absolute counts ("41 intro requests answered this month; top-quartile investors replied within 48 hours") raises investor reply rate relative to a digest without norms, and does not reduce activity among top responders. Segment: the 116 logged-in investors split into two WhatsApp digest lists, alternating variants every two weeks. Metric: 7-day reply rate by investor quartile. Sample and design: 58 per arm is underpowered, so an eight-week crossover (each investor sees both variants) roughly halves variance; still directional. Duration: 8 weeks. Kill: any decline in reply rate among the top quartile of responders, stopped immediately (MovieLens boomerang). Evidence: [Chen, Harper, Konstan & Li 2010](https://mail.yanchen.people.si.umich.edu/papers/soc_pref_movielens_20090619_aer.pdf); [Frey & Meier](https://www.cesifo.org/DocDL/750.pdf); [Goldstein et al. replication](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4118982).

**10. Move the reward from profile completion to first interaction.** Hypothesis: paying the Circle-membership reward for a first 7-day reply (investors) or first published raise plus first intro request (founders) rather than profile completion lifts the share of new activators who take an interaction action in their first 14 days from near zero to at least 25%. Segment: all members activating after launch of the change (about 80 per month at current pace). Metric: first-14-day interaction rate; redemption rate (currently 4 of 61). Sample and design: before/after on two monthly cohorts versus the 79 who activated in the last 30 days and sent zero requests. Duration: 8 weeks. Kill: no change after 100 new activations, or evidence of gaming (replies with no content, requests withdrawn). Evidence: [Kraut & Resnick claim 24](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Kraut10-Contribution-current.pdf); [Anderson et al., badges steer behaviour](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2013/proceedings/p95.pdf); [Freedman & Fraser, foot-in-the-door](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Influence_Compliance/Freedman_Fraser_Foot-in-the-door.pdf).

## 6. Six metrics: three north stars, three guardrails

Marketplace practitioners replace "liquidity" with two measurable signals, match or fill rate and time to match, cut by segment ([a16z, 13 metrics](https://a16z.com/13-metrics-for-marketplace-companies/); [Lenny Rachitsky](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-most-important-marketplace-metrics); [Point Nine](https://writing.pointnine.com/p/wtf-is-marketplace-liquidity)), and community practitioners insist the denominator be activated members, not registrations, since counting dormant accounts is what makes 90-9-1 look true ([FeverBee](https://www.feverbee.com/how-to-treat-the-90-9-1-theory/); [CMX](https://cmxhub.com/1-9-90-theory-of-engagement)). Spinks adds that the value to measure is what members contribute back, not engagement in general ([CMX, Spinks](https://www.cmxhub.com/blog/measure-the-value-of-contribution-not-the-value-of-community)). Target bands below are inferences for the first 90 days, not external benchmarks; none exist for vetted founder-investor networks.

## 7. Risks: spam perception, investor fatigue, dilution and the closed clique

Spam perception is the risk the portal is closest to, because its main channels are a WhatsApp digest and LinkedIn. LinkedIn's contact-import mechanic spiked growth and left "some people with a spammy impression" ([Fast Company](https://www.pressreader.com/usa/fast-company/20230301/283132843002090)); Facebook's 2018 lapsed-user notification emails became a public trust incident ([Boing Boing](https://boingboing.net/2018/07/21/poisoning-the-well.html)); and reciprocity to unsolicited contact fell from 20% to about 2% over forty years as volume rose ([Discover](https://www.discovermagazine.com/mind/only-2-of-people-will-return-a-christmas-card-from-a-stranger)). Mitigations: every reactivation message carries a true, member-specific fact; broadcast volume to dormant accounts is capped and measured against G3; and nothing is sent to the 1,660 dormant accounts that the active 252 could not send personally.

Investor fatigue is the fire that kills a founder-investor network first, in Hoffman's triage sense ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2018/10/01/reid-hoffman-blitzscaling-book/)). Investors "receive many more introduction requests than they can take," and a connector's reputation rides on each forward ([Happenstance](https://happenstance.ai/how-to/double-opt-in-intro)); doubling supply in a time-boxed market cut confirmation rates 15% ([Li & Netessine](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2018.3223)). The portal's own data shows the opposite risk today, 44 requests from 7 senders over ten weeks, so the immediate danger is concentration (a few founders hitting the same investors) rather than volume. Mitigations: a cap of three requests per investor per week, capacity declarations (experiment 2), double opt-in on every brokered intro, and an immediate stop on experiment 9 if top responders slow down. A public reputation score for investors is not recommended; the eBay and Stack Overflow evidence that reputation moves behaviour comes from anonymous, high-volume markets ([Resnick et al.](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/experimental-economics/article/value-of-reputation-on-ebay-a-controlled-experiment/610EA11BA19583907061AFB677BE4338)), and a score could read as status-threatening to senior investors. YC's founder-only investor reviews and Techstars' mentor scoring are the curated-network versions ([YC software](https://ycombinator.com/software); [Techstars](https://www.techstars.com/mentor-faq)).

Dilution arrives in a predictable order: first as a perception among existing members, then as weaker outcomes, then as churn and revenue. On Deck's quality bar slipped once admissions compensation was tied to cohort size ([Boring Business Nerd](https://boringbusinessnerd.com/post/what-i-learned-working-in-a-hypergrowth-startup)); Clubhouse's access-based gate amplified low-intent users faster than fit-based gates ever could ([TNW](https://thenextweb.com/news/clubhouse-opens-to-everyone-ends-invite-only-policy)); Dribbble's invites became a $25–$80 currency ([Intercom](https://www.intercom.com/blog/podcasts/dribbble-ceo-zack-onisko-community/)); Chief's growing waitlist and uneven Core groups became the story ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2023/03/20/chief-womens-network-startup-members-waitlist-valuation-growing-pains/)). Hampton's response was to raise its floor as the brand grew ([Parr on X](https://x.com/thesamparr/status/1829138094064390203)). Mitigations: keep nomination quotas at two per quarter (Gmail's resale market existed at 3–6 invites and vanished at 50, [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Gmail)); keep the published vetting criteria and a member-objection window; never tie team incentives to member count; run the "very disappointed" survey on each admission cohort at 60–90 days so dilution shows up before renewals ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit)); and publish selectivity, as Endeavor (under 7%), Platanus (0.9%) and Hampton (2–8%) do, because scarcity attributed to selection reads as quality while artificial scarcity does not ([Endeavor](https://endeavor.org/?p=428785); [La República](https://www.larepublica.co/globoeconomia/estas-son-las-nuevas-startups-chilenas-seleccionadas-por-platanus-ventures-3728877); [Lynn](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstream/handle/1813/71652/Lynn60_Scarcity_s_enhancement.pdf?sequence=1)).

The mirror-image risk is over-curation into a closed clique. Uzzi's apparel firms gained survival from embedded ties up to a threshold and then lost access to outside information ([Uzzi 1997](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/1997/social-structure-and-competition-in-interfirm-networks-the-paradox)), and professional matching algorithms tend to reinforce existing clusters rather than bridge them ([CACM](https://cacm.acm.org/?p=109017)). The portal's brokerage value depends on moderately weak ties across sub-networks ([Rajkumar et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36107999/)), so a share of nominations should be reserved for investors outside the current member graph, and matching should favour pairs with one or two mutual connections over pairs with many.

## Conclusion

The portal has already done the hardest thing in Chen's framework, collecting the hard side: 100 investors who said yes to deal flow. What it has not done is the thing AngelList, YC and Endeavor all did before any software, which is to put a named operator between each founder and two or three matched investors and refuse to let a first request go unanswered. Treated this way, the 252 activated members are not a small sample to be A/B tested but a concierge book to be worked, and the measurable output is meetings per week and 7-day reply rate inside one US seed-stage slice. Membership growth follows from that loop rather than preceding it: a founder who got a meeting and an investor who got a fundable deal are the only inviters whose nominations will carry the selection effect that keeps vetting pass rates high, and publishing the resulting counts (intros answered, meetings held, selectivity) is the growth marketing. If the reply rate is still under 20% after 60 brokered requests, the matching is wrong and no amount of recruiting will fix it; if it clears 40%, every later experiment has a baseline worth measuring against.
