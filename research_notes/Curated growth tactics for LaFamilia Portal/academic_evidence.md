# Academic evidence on participation, response and contribution in online communities and two-sided professional networks

Scope note: research conducted 2026-11-24 via web search. Most primary-source PDF hosts (kraut.hciresearch.info, nber.org, pubmed, ide.mit.edu, ucsd.edu, umn.edu, acawiki.org, gmu.edu) were unreachable from this session's network, so exact figures below are taken from abstracts, press releases, publisher pages and course summaries surfaced in search. Where a number came from a secondary source or could not be verified, it is flagged. Transfer caveats for a small, high-trust Latino founder/investor network are given under "Inferences" in each section.

## Key Question 1: Kraut & Resnick (2012), "Building Successful Online Communities": design claims on newcomer onboarding, contribution, commitment and norms

### Takeaway
The book distils social-psychology and economics evidence into numbered "design claims." The ones most relevant to reply rates and first-week activation are: ask specific people (not everyone) to do specific tasks they are suited to; show that others have complied; make newcomers' first posts get a reply; recruit via members' social networks; emphasise the number already participating; use mentorship and modest entry barriers to build commitment; and use rewards/status to build attachment.

### Cited Findings
- Book structure: a chapter on "Encouraging contribution" (Kraut & Resnick) and a separate chapter on "The challenges of dealing with newcomers" (Kraut, Burke, Riedl & Resnick); the book is subtitled "Evidence-Based Social Design" (MIT Press, 2012). — [JSTOR table of contents](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt5hhgvw); [USI library record](https://library.usi.edu/record/464247)
- Contribution chapter (draft): under-contribution is a serious problem even in highly successful communities such as Wikipedia. — [Kraut & Resnick contribution chapter draft (GMU course copy)](https://kpmoran.cs.gmu.edu/swe-632-s21/readings/K&R.pdf)
- Design claim (contribution): "Asking specific people to make contributions increases the likelihood that they will" (vs. broadcasting a request to the whole community). — [Kraut, "Encouraging contribution" chapter PDF](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Kraut10-Contribution-current.pdf)
- Design claim (contribution): asking people to perform tasks that interest them and that they are able to perform increases contributions compared with asking people at random. — [Kraut contribution chapter](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Kraut10-Contribution-current.pdf)
- Design claim 12 (contribution): people are more likely to comply with a request when they see that other people have also complied. — [Kraut contribution chapter](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Kraut10-Contribution-current.pdf)
- Design claim 35 (contribution): "People will be more willing to contribute in an online community if they see that others are making complementary or contingent contributions than if they see others making substitute contributions." — [Kraut contribution chapter](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Kraut10-Contribution-current.pdf)
- Design claim 24 (contribution/commitment): "Rewards, whether in the form of status, privileges, or material benefits, will enhance individual liking for and commitment to an online community." — [Kraut contribution chapter](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Kraut10-Contribution-current.pdf)
- Design claim 23 (newcomers): when old-timers provide newcomers formal mentorship, the newcomers become more committed to the community, learn how to behave in it, and contribute more. — [Kraut, "Dealing with newcomers" chapter PDF](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/kraut10-Newcomers-current.pdf)
- Design claim 3 (newcomers): recruiting through current members' social networks increases the numbers who will join. — [Kraut newcomers chapter](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/kraut10-Newcomers-current.pdf)
- Design claim 9 (newcomers): emphasising the number of people already participating in a community will motivate more people to join than will emphasising the community's need. — [Kraut newcomers chapter](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/kraut10-Newcomers-current.pdf)
- Design claims 13 and 17 (newcomers): forcing potential new members to undertake "separating" tasks / entry barriers leads to enhanced commitment among those who complete them (screening plus a commitment/consistency effect). — [Kraut newcomers chapter](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/kraut10-Newcomers-current.pdf)
- The newcomer chapter frames the problem as five sub-problems, starting with recruitment, and organises design claims by which newcomer problem they address (recruitment, selection, retention, socialization, protection). — [HCL Connections wiki summary of the chapter](https://ds-infolib.hcltechsw.com/ldd/lcwiki.nsf/dx/Dealing_with_Newcomers_in_Online_Communities)
- Evidence cited in the newcomer chapter — Joyce & Kraut (2006), JCMC 11(3): initial posts by 2,777 newcomers to six Usenet newsgroups; newcomers whose first post received a reply were substantially more likely to post again. A secondary summary reports ~61% of newcomers received a reply and that a reply raised the probability of posting again from 44% to 56% (+12 points) — figures not verified against the full paper. — [Joyce & Kraut 2006 (ACM DOI)](https://dx.doi.org/10.1145%2F1124772.1124916); [scite.ai summary](https://scite.ai/authors/elisabeth-joyce-ZGKALM)
- Evidence cited in the newcomer chapter — Burke, Marlow & Lento (CHI 2009), "Feed me": two-thirds of new Facebook members never post in their first two weeks; seeing friends actively contribute predicts later sharing; singling out newcomers appears to affect only those not initially inclined to share. — [Stanford CS278 lecture citing the paper](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs278/lectures/02-ghosttowns.pdf); [Facebook Research listing](https://research.fb.com/publications/feed-me-motivating-newcomer-contribution-in-social-network-sites/)
- Evidence cited in the newcomer chapter — Halfaker, Geiger, Morgan & Riedl (2013), American Behavioral Scientist 57(5): Wikipedia's editor decline is driven by falling retention of "desirable, good-faith" newcomers, whose inflow has been flat since 2006; the share of desirable newcomers reverted in their first session rose, retention of undesirable newcomers stayed constant, and formal norm-setting became resistant to changes proposed by newer editors. Builds on Halfaker et al. (2011) finding that rejection reduces newcomer retention. — [Wikimedia Research summary](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Research:The_Rise_and_Decline); [UMN news](https://cse.umn.edu/college/news/wikipedia-inadvertently-causing-its-own-decline-participation)
- Evidence cited in the contribution chapter — Chen, Harper, Konstan & Li (2010), AER 100(4): personalised social-comparison emails on MovieLens (see KQ3 for figures). — [AER record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:100:y:2010:i:4:p:1358-98)

### Inferences
- For an intro-request product, the two highest-leverage claims are (a) route each request to a named, well-matched recipient rather than a pool, and (b) guarantee the requester gets some reply, since the Usenet and Wikipedia evidence both show that a first interaction without response (or with a rejection) is the dominant churn point.
- "Emphasise how many already participate, not how much we need you" argues for showing "X investors replied this week" rather than "we need more investor replies."
- Transfer caveat: the book's evidence base is large, anonymous, open communities (Usenet, Wikipedia, MovieLens, Facebook). In a small, curated, identity-visible professional network, baseline reply propensity is likely much higher and social-loafing/diffusion-of-responsibility effects smaller, so effect sizes should be treated as directional, not literal.

### Gaps
- Full verbatim list of all design claims with the specific studies behind each could not be retrieved; the chapter PDFs (kraut.hciresearch.info) and acawiki summary were unreachable from this session. Claim numbering above follows the chapter-draft PDFs and may differ from the published book.
- The 44%→56% Joyce & Kraut figure is from a secondary summary and unverified.

## Key Question 2: Response-rate visibility and reputation (Airbnb, Upwork/oDesk, Fiverr, eBay, Stack Overflow, dating markets)

### Takeaway
Visible responsiveness and reputation reliably move counterpart behaviour: a strong eBay reputation earns ~8% price premium; on Fiverr each hour of reply delay cuts hiring odds ~46% and a >24h reply is equivalent to no reply; on oDesk a capacity/availability signal raised invitations, acceptance and hiring; on Airbnb roughly half of inquiries are rejected or ignored and rejected guests leave the platform; in dating, removing the "who viewed me" signal (anonymity) cut matches ~14% for women. Reputation on Stack Overflow correlates with answer acceptance, and a small randomised reputation boost raised contribution without lowering quality, mainly among low-reputation users.

### Cited Findings
- eBay: Resnick, Zeckhauser, Swanson & Lockwood (2006), Experimental Economics — first randomised controlled field experiment on an internet reputation system; matched pairs of vintage postcards sold under an established seller's very high reputation ID vs. newcomer IDs; buyers' willingness to pay was 8.1% higher for the high-reputation seller (press/HKS versions report 7.6%); one or two negative feedbacks on new IDs had no price effect. — [Cambridge/Experimental Economics](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/experimental-economics/article/value-of-reputation-on-ebay-a-controlled-experiment/610EA11BA19583907061AFB677BE4338); [HKS abstract](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/value-reputation-ebay-controlled-experiment); [UMich news](https://news.umich.edu/?p=4919)
- Fiverr response speed: "Speed Is a Signal: When Faster Replies Increase Hiring Likelihood" (Management Science, 2024/25) — ~11.66M Fiverr transactions plus 3 main experiments (n=3,603) and 5 supplemental (n=5,039). A 5-minute delay lowers hiring odds ~15%; a 1-hour delay cuts them ~46%; replying after >24h was equivalent to not replying. Speed mattered independently of ratings (even with no/low ratings) and even when only one freelancer was contacted; the advantage disappears if the reply looks automated/AI-generated; content can partially compensate for modest delay. — [Management Science DOI](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2024.06185); [UCSD news](https://today.ucsd.edu/story/looking-for-work-dont-worry-about-seeming-too-eager); [Vanderbilt news](https://business.vanderbilt.edu/news/2026/07/23/go-ahead-and-reply-to-that-email-right-now-it-may-get-you-hired/)
- oDesk capacity signal: Horton (2019), Management Science 65(8), "Buyer Uncertainty About Seller Capacity" — employers often approach workers with little spare capacity who reject inquiries, lowering fill probability; a feature letting workers signal high availability led signalers to receive more invitations, reject a smaller fraction, quote lower prices and be hired more; estimated market surplus gain up to 6%. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v65y2019i8p3518-3540.html); [Horton paper page](https://john-joseph-horton.com/papers/buyer-uncertainty-about-seller-capacity-causes-consequences-and-a-partial-solution/)
- oDesk public evaluations: Pallais (2014), AER 104(11) — 3,767 low-wage applicants in experimental sample; 952 randomly selected workers hired and given either detailed or coarse public evaluations; both being hired and receiving more detailed public evaluations substantially improved subsequent employment outcomes; market-level benefits exceeded cost (with a displacement caveat). — [NBER w18917](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18917); [AER record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:104:y:2014:i:11:p:3565-99)
- Airbnb inquiries: Fradkin (2015/2017 working papers) — a 2015 version cited by others reports 49% of inquiries rejected or ignored and only 15% leading to a transaction; rejected searchers are more likely to leave Airbnb; rejections decomposed into stale vacancies, congestion (rare) and screening; counterfactual: without availability tracking/filtering, successful matches would fall ~68% and rejections rise ~140% (2015 conference version: removing frictions yields ~102% more matches). Figures come from different versions and secondary citations. — [Fradkin, Search/Matching paper (MIT IDE)](https://ide.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/SearchMatchingEfficiency.pdf?x41178); [IDE research brief](https://ide.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/IDE-Research-Brief_0717.pdf?x19295); [2015 conference version](https://eudl.eu/doi/10.4108/eai.8-8-2015.2260850)
- Airbnb response rate metric: Airbnb defines host response rate as share of inquiries/requests answered within 24h; vendor (non-academic) data claims conversion of 0.5% for low-response hosts vs 1.0% for 100% responders and 1.0% for <1h replies vs 0.8% slower — correlational, source data unstated. — [Intellihost blog](https://intellihost.co/how-response-rate-and-response-time-affect-your-bookings/)
- Airbnb reviews: Fradkin & Holtz — incentivised review policy raised review rate 53% relative to control and induced lower average star ratings; authors disclose financial interests in Airbnb. — [Marketing Science supplement](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/suppl/10.1287/mksc.2021.1311/suppl_file/mksc.2021.1311.sm1.pdf)
- Stack Overflow badges: Anderson, Huttenlocher, Kleinberg & Leskovec (WWW 2013), "Steering User Behavior with Badges" — badges increased overall participation and shifted effort toward badge-earning actions (e.g., Electorate badge/voting); later work (Hoernle et al. 2020; Yanovsky et al. JASIST 2021) finds steering is less widespread than first thought and most users appear apathetic to badges. — [WWW 2013 PDF](https://archives.iw3c2.org/www2013/proceedings/p95.pdf); [Hoernle et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.06160.pdf); [Yanovsky et al.](https://tzin.bgu.ac.il/~omerlev/papers/jasist21.pdf)
- Stack Overflow reputation: user reputation is among the strongest predictors of an answer being accepted (correlational); a PlatStrat 2025 field experiment that randomly gave contributors a small anonymous upvote found the largest effect for low-to-moderate reputation users and no reduction in quality or effort. — [Feature study (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.02830); [PlatStrat 2025 paper](https://questromworld.bu.edu/platformstrategy/wp-content/uploads/sites/49/2025/07/PlatStrat2025_paper_140.pdf)
- Stack Overflow unanswered questions: pushing questions from zero/negative-reputation users into chat rooms raised the chance of an answer within two hours by 5.9 percentage points. — [NBER conference paper](https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f152278/f152278.pdf)
- Dating — Bapna, Ramaprasad, Shmueli & Umyarov (2016), Management Science 62(11), "One-Way Mirrors": 50,000 of 100,000 randomly selected new users on a major North American dating site given anonymous browsing; anonymous users viewed more profiles (more same-sex and interracial) but, having lost the "weak signal" of a visible profile view, ended with fewer matches (≥3 messages exchanged); effect strongest for women, who rarely send first messages; press release reports ~14% fewer matches for anonymous women. — [Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2015.2301); [McGill release](https://www.mcgill.ca/newsroom/node/25218); [Carlson School release](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/news/new-research-suggests-anonymous-browsing-hinders-online-dating-signals)
- Dating — Hitsch, Hortaçsu & Ariely (2010), "What Makes You Click?" QME 8(4) and "Matching and Sorting in Online Dating" AER 100(1): men viewed >3x more profiles and sent >3x more first-contact messages than women; the number of first contacts a user receives reveals their "type"; no evidence of strategic (self-handicapping) behaviour; strong preference for similarity. — [QME record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:kap:qmktec:v:8:y:2010:i:4:p:393-427); [Duke working paper](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/52e2e703-3736-4e0b-a1db-5e0282062f9d/content)

### Inferences
- The Fiverr and oDesk results together suggest that for intro requests the most valuable visible signals are (1) responsiveness/speed and (2) current capacity ("open to 2 intros this month"), not just a static reputation score. Making "typical reply time" visible and defaulting a capacity signal are directly supported designs.
- Bapna et al. implies that letting a member see "X viewed your profile" is a low-cost weak signal that raises two-way conversations, especially for the side that is reluctant to initiate (likely founders approaching investors).
- Fradkin's Airbnb work shows the churn cost of non-response is on the requester side: a founder whose first intro request is ignored is the analogue of a rejected Airbnb guest who leaves.
- Transfer caveats: eBay/Fiverr/Airbnb are anonymous, high-volume, price-mediated markets where reputation substitutes for personal trust. In a curated, identity-verified community, reputation scores may matter less and social-norm/reciprocity levers more; public reputation scores could also feel status-threatening to senior investors and depress participation (no direct evidence either way found).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of Airbnb host response rate/time → booking conversion was found; only vendor blogs.
- Hitsch et al. first-contact reply-rate percentages were not retrievable (full text blocked).
- No academic study of Upwork's "response rate"/"responsiveness" badge was found; the Fiverr paper is the closest causal evidence.

## Key Question 3: Reciprocity, social proof and commitment (Cialdini; Goldstein et al.; Salganik; Frey & Meier; Chen et al. 2010)

### Takeaway
Descriptive-norm messages ("most people like you do X") produce real but modest field effects (+9 points towel reuse; conditional cooperation among Zurich students), while personalised social-comparison feedback can produce very large shifts (MovieLens: +530% ratings among below-median users, but −62% among above-median). Reciprocity effects are robust in the lab (Regan 1971) and in the field (20% of strangers returned an unsolicited Christmas card in 1976, though only ~2% in a 2015 replication). Social influence also amplifies inequality and unpredictability of what becomes popular (Salganik et al.).

### Cited Findings
- Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius (2008), Journal of Consumer Research 35(3), "A Room with a Viewpoint": hotel towel-reuse field experiment. Bohner & Schlüter's (2014) replication paper reports the original's descriptive-norm card achieved 44.1% reuse vs 35.1% for the standard environmental appeal; Stanford SPARQ summarises standard placards at 37% vs the "provincial" (same-room) norm at 50%; APS describes the descriptive-norm message as yielding ~48%. The 2014 replication found only the general norm, not the provincial norm, beat control, so the provincial-norm advantage is contested. — [Bohner & Schlüter 2014 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4118982); [Stanford SPARQ](https://sparq.stanford.edu/solutions/simple-message-saves-water); [APS Observer](https://psychologicalscience.org/?p=47279)
- Frey & Meier (2004), JEBO 54 ("Pro-Social Behavior in a Natural Setting") / "Social Comparisons and Pro-social Behavior: Testing Conditional Cooperation in a Field Experiment": every University of Zurich student decides each semester whether to pay into two social funds; 2,500 randomly selected students were told either that a high or a relatively low share of students contributed; students shown the high reference rate contributed more than those shown the low rate (conditional cooperation); actual contribution rate was 67%, which students underestimated. Exact treatment percentages not retrievable (full text blocked). — [CESifo WP 750](https://www.cesifo.org/DocDL/750.pdf); [IEW WP 162](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:zur:iewwpx:162); [UCSD-hosted PDF](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreoni/PhilanthropyAndFundraising/Volume%202/23%20Frey%20Meier%202004.pdf)
- Follow-up (Meier, "Does framing matter for conditional cooperation?"): telling students "X% contributed" vs "equivalently (100−X)% did not contribute" made limited difference — framing effects are limited. — [EconBiz record](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/does-framing-matter-for-conditional-cooperation-evidence-from-a-natural-field-experiment/10011038957)
- Chen, Harper, Konstan & Li (2010), AER 100(4):1358–98, MovieLens field experiment (~400 users): personalised email newsletters compared each user's activity with the community median; below-median users increased monthly ratings by 530%, above-median users decreased monthly ratings by 62% — both converged toward the median (conformity to a newly salient norm); above-average users told about the average net-benefit score shifted toward activities that help others. — [AER/RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:100:y:2010:i:4:p:1358-98); [IGERT highlight](https://igert.org/highlights/368.html); [Working paper PDF](https://mail.yanchen.people.si.umich.edu/papers/soc_pref_movielens_20090619_aer.pdf)
- Salganik, Dodds & Watts (2006), Science 311:854–856, "Music Lab": 14,341 participants downloaded unknown songs with or without seeing prior download counts; social influence increased both inequality and unpredictability of success in all eight influence worlds relative to the independent condition. — [Paper PDF](https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/research/papers/years/2006/salganik2006a.pdf); [Princeton project page](https://www.princeton.edu/~mjs3/musiclab.shtml)
- Regan (1971), reciprocity: participants given an unsolicited Coke by a confederate later bought roughly twice as many raffle tickets from him as controls, regardless of liking — figure from a secondary source. — [NPR summary](https://www.nhpr.org/national/2012-11-26/give-and-take-how-the-rule-of-reciprocation-binds-us); [Adobe Express summary (low quality)](https://new.express.adobe.com/page/l4Ssq)
- Kunz & Woolcott (1976): Christmas cards mailed to 578 strangers; 117 (20%) replied; higher-status sender ("Dr. and Mrs.") drew 26% replies vs 15% for plain names. Kunz's 2000 replication again ~20%; Meier's 2015 replication only ~2%, attributed to unsolicited mail now reading as spam. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/psysociety/i-8217-ll-show-you-my-holiday-card-if-you-show-me-yours/); [Discover](https://www.discovermagazine.com/mind/only-2-of-people-will-return-a-christmas-card-from-a-stranger)
- Freedman & Fraser (1966), JPSP 4(2):195–202, foot-in-the-door: agreeing to a small request first raised compliance with a large request versus a large-request-only control (exact percentages not surfaced in search; commonly cited as ~17% vs ~76% for the safe-driving sign study — unverified here); replicated by Gamian-Wilk & Dolinski (2020). — [MIT course copy of paper](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Influence_Compliance/Freedman_Fraser_Foot-in-the-door.pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5969145/)
- Design-claim synthesis: Kraut & Resnick design claim 12 (seeing others comply raises compliance) and claim 9 (emphasise numbers participating, not need) encode these findings for community design. — [Kraut contribution chapter](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Kraut10-Contribution-current.pdf)

### Inferences
- The MovieLens boomerang (above-median users cut activity by 62%) is the most important warning for a small network: a public "average reply rate" can pull high responders down. Use upward-only comparisons ("you're in the top 20% of responders") or compare to a high reference group, as Frey & Meier's high-rate treatment did.
- Reciprocity's status effect (26% vs 15%) suggests intro requests that arrive via a high-status or known mutual contact will get more replies than otherwise identical cold requests, consistent with practitioner warm-intro data (KQ8).
- The collapse from 20% to 2% in Christmas-card reciprocity over 40 years shows that unsolicited outreach norms decay as volume rises; a curated platform's value is partly keeping request volume low enough that a request still feels personal.
- Transfer caveat: norm messages work best when the reference group is similar and salient (provincial norm). "Latino founders/investors like you" is a plausibly strong reference group, but the provincial-norm advantage failed to replicate in Bohner & Schlüter (2014), so test before relying on it.

### Gaps
- Exact Frey & Meier treatment contribution rates and the exact Goldstein et al. 2008 condition percentages (from the original paper) could not be verified.
- No study was found that tests reciprocity nudges specifically on reply rates to professional introduction requests.

## Key Question 4: Defaults and choice architecture (Thaler & Sunstein; Johnson & Goldstein; Madrian & Shea)

### Takeaway
Defaults produce some of the largest effects in behavioural science: opt-out organ-donation framing raised stated consent from 42% to 82%, and automatic 401(k) enrollment raised new-hire participation from 37% to 86% (+48 points), with effects largely independent of the default contribution rate.

### Cited Findings
- Johnson & Goldstein (2003, Science; 2004, Transplantation): online experiment — 42% consent when default was "not a donor," 82% when default was "donor," 79% in a neutral (forced-choice) condition; the neutral condition was not significantly different from opt-out. Cross-country, opt-out (presumed-consent) countries had much higher apparent agreement and statistically significantly higher actual donation rates with controls. — [Johnson & Goldstein 2004 PDF](https://www.dangoldstein.com/papers/JohnsonGoldstein_Defaults_Transplantation2004.pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15614141/); [AMA Journal of Ethics review](https://journalofethics.ama-assn.org/article/does-how-we-ask-organs-determine-whether-people-decide-donate/2005-09)
- Madrian & Shea (2001), QJE 116(4), "The Power of Suggestion": at a large U.S. corporation, automatic enrollment raised 401(k) participation among employees in tenure months 3–15 from 37% to 86% (+48 points) and overall participation by 11 points after fifteen months; later work found the effect did not depend on whether the default rate was 3% or 6%. — [NBER Reporter 2024](https://nber.org/reporter/2024number3/influencing-retirement-savings-decisions-automatic-enrollment-and-related-tools); [Boston College CRR](https://crr.bc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/How-Helpful-Is-Auto-Enrollment-in-401k-Plans_-–-Center-for-Retirement-Research.pdf); [NBER w7682](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7682.pdf)
- Default effects persist: participants under automatic enrollment also cluster at the default contribution rate and fund, i.e., the default shapes not just participation but the level of engagement. — [CEPR/VoxEU column](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/auto-enrollment-changes-who-passive-saver)

### Inferences
- Applied to intro requests: default recipients into "accepting intros, 2 per month" with an easy opt-out, default-on reply reminders, and a default reply template (pre-filled "yes / not now / refer to someone else") are the closest analogues; the neutral forced-choice condition (79%) suggests that simply requiring an explicit yes/no at onboarding captures most of the default gain without feeling coercive.
- Transfer caveat: default effects are strongest when the action is low-stakes, infrequent, and inertia-prone (one-time enrollment). Replying to a specific human's request is a recurring, socially visible act; defaults can lower friction but cannot substitute for motivation, and a default "yes" that members silently ignore would damage requester trust more than an honest "not now."

### Gaps
- Original Madrian & Shea paper and Thaler & Sunstein's Nudge were not directly accessed; figures are from NBER/CRR summaries.
- No field experiment on defaults in intro/networking products was found.

## Key Question 5: Scarcity and exclusivity effects (Lynn 1991; Cialdini; Verhallen & Robben)

### Takeaway
Scarcity raises perceived value, but the average effect is small (meta-analytic r ≈ 0.12 in Lynn 1991) and highly heterogeneous; scarcity due to popularity (demand) tends to be read as a quality signal, whereas accidental or supply-driven unavailability works differently. Effects are strongest for people high in need for uniqueness.

### Cited Findings
- Lynn (1991), Psychology & Marketing 8(1):43–57, "Scarcity Effects on Value: A Quantitative Review of the Commodity Theory Literature": meta-analysis of 41 studies, 51 scarcity main effects and 11 scarcity × need-for-uniqueness interactions; small average effect with significant heterogeneity, moderated by study attributes (e.g., tangible vs intangible commodity). Barton, Zlatevska & Oppewal (2022) cite Lynn's perceived-value effect as r = 0.12. — [Cornell eCommons record](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/entities/publication/167c0da3-fa55-44ad-be11-f258914ee9a3); [Lynn PDF](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstream/handle/1813/71652/Lynn60_Scarcity_s_enhancement.pdf?sequence=1); [Barton et al. 2022 summary](https://pure.bond.edu.au/ws/portalfiles/portal/182976505/Scarcity_tactics_in_marketing.pdf)
- Barton, Zlatevska & Oppewal (2022), Journal of Retailing: newer meta-analysis of 416 effect sizes from 131 studies extending Lynn; heterogeneity explained by between-study moderators (scarcity type, product type). — [Bond University PDF](https://pure.bond.edu.au/ws/portalfiles/portal/182976505/Scarcity_tactics_in_marketing.pdf)
- Verhallen & Robben (1994), Journal of Economic Psychology 15(2):315–331: experiment with four availability conditions — unlimited; limited due to popularity; limited due to limited supply; accidental unavailability — testing how the attributed cause of scarcity changes product evaluation; Verhallen (1982) found scarce goods desired more than available ones. — [EconBiz record](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/scarcity-and-preference-an-experiment-on-unavailability-and-product-evaluation-verhallen-theo/10005383497); [Tilburg repository](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/items/f7d2592f-fb70-4047-9a3f-3213c32b6d4b)
- Kraut & Resnick design claims 13/17: entry barriers ("separating tasks") raise commitment among those who complete them — the community-design version of scarcity/exclusivity. — [Kraut newcomers chapter](https://kraut.hciresearch.info/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/kraut10-Newcomers-current.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a curated network, exclusivity is more defensible as a quality/screening signal ("limited due to popularity/selection") than as artificial supply scarcity; the literature suggests the former is read as a quality cue and the latter can backfire if perceived as manipulative.
- Capping intro requests (e.g., N per month) is scarcity applied to the requester side; it should raise the perceived value of each request and, by reducing volume, protect reply norms (see Christmas-card decay in KQ3).
- Transfer caveat: the scarcity literature is about consumer goods evaluated in isolation; effect sizes (r≈0.12) are small and should not be the primary growth lever.

### Gaps
- Verhallen & Robben's specific results by condition were not retrievable.
- Cialdini's Influence (scarcity chapter) is a trade synthesis; no new primary evidence beyond the above was located.

## Key Question 6: Network structure and value (Granovetter; Burt; Rajkumar et al. 2022; Uzzi)

### Takeaway
Weak ties and brokerage across structural holes create value by carrying non-redundant information: Granovetter's job-changers got 56% of jobs via contacts and 84% of those via weak ties; Burt's brokers were better paid, better evaluated, more promoted and produced ideas rated more valuable; the 20-million-user LinkedIn experiment confirms causally that moderately weak ties raise job mobility most (inverted-U), especially in digital industries. Uzzi shows embeddedness (strong ties) boosts survival up to a threshold, after which it harms.

### Cited Findings
- Granovetter (1973), AJS, and Getting a Job (1974): sample of 282 professional, technical and managerial men in a Boston suburb; 56% found their job through a personal contact; of those, 84% found it through a contact seen "occasionally" or "rarely"; breakdown among contact-users — 16.7% saw the contact often (strong tie), 55.6% occasionally, 27.8% rarely. Weak ties matter most for professional occupations. — [Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2023/07/strength-weak-ties); [PMI summary](https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/viewpoints-career-opportunities-networking-3266); [Granovetter 2005 JEP](https://cpi.stanford.edu/_media/pdf/Reference%20Media/Granovetter_2005_Social%20Networks.pdf)
- Burt (2004), AJS 110(2), "Structural Holes and Good Ideas": supply-chain managers at a large U.S. electronics firm (secondary sources cite 673 managers; a Berkeley handout cites 455 in the idea-value regression); compensation, positive performance evaluations, promotions and good ideas were disproportionately held by people whose networks span structural holes (low network constraint); brokers were more likely to express ideas, less likely to have ideas dismissed, more likely to have ideas rated valuable. — [Burt 2004 PDF (Stanford SNAP)](https://snap.stanford.edu/class/cs224w-readings/Burt04StructureHole.pdf); [Berkeley seminar handout](https://businessinnovation.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/businessinnovation-archive/WilliamsonSeminar/burt012606handout.pdf); [UChicago Magazine](https://magazine.uchicago.edu/0408/research/business.shtml)
- Rajkumar, Saint-Jacques, Bojinov, Brynjolfsson & Aral (2022), Science 377(6612), "A causal test of the strength of weak ties": multiple large randomised experiments on LinkedIn's People You May Know algorithm varying the share of weak-tie recommendations; >20 million users over 5 years; 2 billion new ties and 600,000 new jobs recorded; weak ties increased job mobility more than strong ties, with an inverted-U (moderately weak ties, measured by mutual connections and interaction intensity, helped most; the very weakest showed diminishing returns); weak ties raised mobility in more digital industries, strong ties in less digital ones. — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36107999/); [Stanford Digital Economy Lab](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/a-causal-test-of-the-strength-of-weak-ties); [MIT Sloan release](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/node/33013); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/a-massive-linkedin-study-reveals-who-actually-helps-you-get-that-job/)
- Uzzi (1996), ASR, and (1997), ASQ "Paradox of Embeddedness": ethnography of 23 New York apparel firms; firms organised in embedded networks had higher survival than those with arm's-length ties, but the benefit reversed past a threshold — over-embeddedness insulates firms from outside information and exposes them to shocks. — [Kellogg 1996 record](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/1996/the-sources-and-consequences-of-embeddedness-for-the-economic/); [Kellogg 1997 record](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/1997/social-structure-and-competition-in-interfirm-networks-the-paradox)
- Professional social matching tends to reinforce existing clusters rather than bridge to unknown communities; algorithms trained on past connections can strengthen human biases. — [CACM review](https://cacm.acm.org/?p=109017)

### Inferences
- The core value proposition of a curated Latino founder–investor network is brokerage: it manufactures moderately weak ties (shared identity/community = some mutual connections and trust; different sub-networks = non-redundant information). Rajkumar's inverted-U suggests the sweet spot is introductions between members with a few mutual connections, not total strangers and not close friends.
- Uzzi's threshold warns against letting the community become a closed clique; deliberately seeding bridges to non-Latino investor networks preserves the information advantage.
- Transfer caveat: Rajkumar's effects are on job applications/transitions at LinkedIn scale; the mechanism (information diffusion across weak ties) should hold in a small network, but the magnitude cannot be extrapolated.

### Gaps
- Exact effect sizes (percentage change in job transitions per weak tie; the mutual-connection cutoff) from the Science paper were not retrievable; press coverage did not report them.
- Burt's compensation coefficients could not be extracted.

## Key Question 7: Two-sided platform research (Parker, Van Alstyne & Choudary; Hagiu & Wright; congestion and curated matching — Horton, Fradkin, Li & Netessine)

### Takeaway
Platform theory says value comes from enabling direct interactions between distinct user groups, and that uncurated growth creates negative network effects via congestion and low-quality participants. Empirically, doubling market size on a rental platform cut traveler confirmation 15.4% and host occupancy 15.9% (Li & Netessine); algorithmic recruiting recommendations raised oDesk fill rates ~20% without displacing other hires (Horton); and a capacity signal raised acceptance and hiring (Horton 2019).

### Cited Findings
- Hagiu & Wright (2015), IJIO 43:162–174, "Multi-Sided Platforms": an MSP "creates value primarily by enabling direct interactions between at least two distinct types of affiliated customers"; cross-group network effects are neither necessary nor sufficient; MSP status does not guarantee profitability. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:indorg:v:43:y:2015:i:c:p:162-174); [HBS Working Knowledge](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/6681.html)
- Parker, Van Alstyne & Choudary (2016), Platform Revolution, and Van Alstyne, Parker & Choudary (HBR 2016): negative same-side and cross-side network effects arise when poor curation degrades experience; if a platform is too open, "poor quality contributions or misbehavior of some participants ... causes others to defect"; OkCupid cited as an example where uncurated growth overwhelmed users and led to multi-level matching. — [HBR "Pipelines, Platforms..." (course copy)](https://courses.edx.org/asset-v1:BUx+QD505x+2T2017+type@asset+block@6_Reasons_Platforms_Fail_-_HBR.org.pdf); [SuperSummary of the book](https://www.supersummary.com/platform-revolution/summary/)
- Li & Netessine (2020), Management Science 66(1):271–289: exogenous one-time migration of listings onto a peer-to-peer holiday-rental platform; doubling market size associated with 15.4% lower traveler confirmation rate, 15.9% lower host occupancy, ~5.6% of potential matches lost; traveler search intensity rose 18.3%; effect strongest when matching must occur within a limited time window. — [Wharton Mack Institute summary](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/?p=18190); [Management Science](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2018.3223)
- Horton (2017), Journal of Labor Economics, "The Effects of Algorithmic Labor Market Recommendations": oDesk field experiment; employers receiving algorithmic candidate recommendations filled openings at a ~20% higher rate than control; no displacement of non-recommended hires; recommended recruits positively selected; effect largest for openings that would otherwise draw small applicant pools (earlier 2012 draft: effect concentrated in technical vacancies). — [JOLE record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:jlabec:doi:10.1086/689213); [Horton paper page](https://john-joseph-horton.com/papers/the-effects-of-algorithmic-labor-market-recommendations-evidence-from-a-field-experiment/)
- Barach, Kaul, Leung & Lu (Upwork): firms rely on platform AI recommendations when deciding which applicants to view but largely ignore them when choosing among interviewed candidates; reliance weaker for specialised jobs and experienced employers. — [Wharton Mack Institute PDF](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Barach-Moshe-Kaul-Aseem-Leung-Ming-and-Lu-Sibo_Small-Numbers-Bargaining-in-the-Age-of-Big-Data.-Evidence-from-a-Two-Sided-Labor-Matching-Platform.pdf)
- Horton (2019), Management Science: capacity-signalling feature raised invitations, acceptance and hires; up to 6% surplus gain (see KQ2). — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v65y2019i8p3518-3540.html)
- Fradkin (2017): Airbnb rejections largely from stale vacancies and host screening; availability tracking/filtering prevents ~68% fall in matches (see KQ2). — [MIT IDE PDF](https://ide.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/SearchMatchingEfficiency.pdf?x41178)
- Bana & Boudreau (IZA 2023): job-matching platform field experiment, 4,534 subjects randomised to be told or not told that an algorithm produced their recommendation; disclosure alone shifted outcomes. — [IZA conference paper](https://conference.iza.org/DATA_2023/bana_s26582.pdf)

### Inferences
- Li & Netessine is the strongest empirical case for curation: more supply does not mean more matches when search is costly and time-boxed. For intro requests, routing (recommend 2–3 best-fit investors) beats a browsable directory.
- Horton's 20% fill-rate lift from recommendations, concentrated where applicant pools are thin, maps well onto a small network where any given founder has few plausible investors: concierge/algorithmic suggestions should matter most for under-networked members.
- Transfer caveat: these platforms optimise one-off transactions; a professional community also optimises repeated relationships, so "matching efficiency" should be measured on conversations-that-continue, not on first replies alone.

### Gaps
- Platform Revolution's own text on curation was not accessible; claims are via HBR article and summaries.
- No peer-reviewed evidence found on optimal request caps or queue limits for professional intro platforms.

## Key Question 8: Evidence that concierge or algorithmic matching increases match rates in professional networking (Lunchclub, Weave, Bumble Bizz, double opt-in intros)

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed or large field-experimental evaluation of Lunchclub, Weave or Bumble Bizz was found. The closest rigorous evidence is LinkedIn's PYMK experiments (Rajkumar et al.) and oDesk recommendation experiments (Horton), both showing algorithmic introductions causally increase downstream outcomes. Practitioner data on warm vs cold investor intros show large gaps (roughly 40–80% vs 1–12% reply) but come from vendors, and the "double opt-in" step is a practitioner norm, not a measured intervention.

### Cited Findings
- Lunchclub: self-described AI matcher for weekly 1:1 video meetings; users state background, goals and interests and opt in weekly; matching is proprietary and cannot be audited externally; user reviews mixed (frustration over lack of control). No independent study located. — [HP Tech@Work review](https://www.hp.com/us-en/shop/tech-takes/lunch-club-networking); [Product Hunt](https://www.producthunt.com/products/lunchclub)
- Computational social matching in organisations (Tampere): design-research work on algorithmic matching to increase organisational fluidity; no effect sizes on match rates. — [Tampere postprint](https://trepo.tuni.fi/bitstream/10024/129865/1/Facilitating_Organizational_Fluidity_with_Computational_Social_Matching_final_postprint.pdf)
- CACM review of professional social matching: algorithms can entrench bias via training data; people naturally reinforce existing clusters rather than bridge to unknown communities. — [CACM](https://cacm.acm.org/?p=109017)
- Causal algorithmic-introduction evidence (adjacent): LinkedIn PYMK experiments (KQ6) and oDesk recommendations +20% fill (KQ7). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36107999/); [JOLE](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:jlabec:doi:10.1086/689213)
- Warm vs cold investor outreach (practitioner/vendor, not peer-reviewed): one benchmark gives cold outreach 1–5% response and <1% meeting vs warm intro 50–80% response and 25–50% meeting; Round Funded (2026) gives personalised cold email 8–12% vs warm intro via portfolio founder 40–50%, and notes "would you be open to chat"-style intros convert at 15–25% vs 40–60% for endorsement-style intros. — [Metal.so](https://www.metal.so/collections/warm-intros-vs-cold-email-2025-conversion-data-referral-playbook); [Round Funded](https://www.roundfunded.com/en/blogs/warm-intro-vs-cold-email-vcs-40-percent-vs-2-percent-2026)
- Double opt-in intros: described by practitioners (Mauskopf; Startups.com) as asking the investor to opt in before forwarding the founder's email; SaaStr argues that without double qualification a warm intro can be worse than a cold email because declining carries social cost. No measured effect found. — [Inc. (Mauskopf)](https://www.inc.com/sara-mauskopf/why-you-should-stop-asking-your-network-for-warm-intros.html); [SaaStr](https://www.saastr.com/dear-saastr-why-are-vcs-so-adamant-about-warm-intros)

### Inferences
- The absence of evaluations means a curated platform should instrument its own A/B tests; the oDesk and LinkedIn designs (randomise whether a member receives a recommended intro vs browses) are directly replicable at small scale.
- The endorsement-vs-"open to chat" gap in vendor data is consistent with the Fiverr/eBay signal literature: intros that carry a substantive quality signal outperform neutral connection requests.

### Gaps
- No randomized or peer-reviewed study of Lunchclub, Weave, Bumble Bizz or any concierge-intro service was found.
- All warm-intro reply-rate figures are from vendors with commercial interests; none report sample sizes or methods.
