Findings below are therefore drawn from search-result excerpts of those pages plus one successful fetch (the Orbit Model GitHub repo). ## KQ1. Community metric frameworks: CMX SPACES, Community Industry Report, Orbit model, Commsor, Spinks, FeverBee/Millington and the 90-9-1 rule

### Takeaway
The community-industry frameworks (SPACES, Orbit, Commsor/Common Room) are mostly *taxonomies* for tying activity to business value and for segmenting members by depth of involvement; they publish very few hard numeric benchmarks. The one numeric heuristic everyone cites, 90-9-1, is widely criticized (including by Millington and CMX's own Slack data) as not applying to small communities, and the critics' data put "active" shares anywhere from ~7% (CMX Slack) to 23-45% (Higher Logic, Airbyte).

### Cited Findings
- SPACES stands for Support, Product, Acquisition, Content, Engagement (internal and external), Success; CMX built it to "categorize the business objectives their work supports" and suggests going pillar by pillar asking whether the team is active there and what evidence shows success — [CMX: SPACES framework in action](https://www.cmxhub.com/blog/spaces-framework-in-action-match-your-community-work-to-business-goals); [CMX: Build a community measurement strategy](https://cmxhub.com/build-a-community-measurement-strategy-you-can-be-proud-of)
- SPACES gives example metrics rather than one fixed list; for Acquisition a CMX-affiliated post suggests new users, active users, conversion and sign-ups; CMX recommends a measurement sequence that starts from the business value the community should drive — [CMX: The simple 3-step model for measuring community](https://cmxhub.com/community-measurement-strategy/); [C. Mitchell Adams: The SPACES model](https://cmitchelladams.squarespace.com/thoughts/the-spaces-model-for-community)
- Spinks (CMX founder, author of *The Business of Belonging*) argues the value is in what members *contribute* back, not in engagement per se: "community programming exists for members, and the business value shows up in what members contribute back"; trying to pull a dollar figure out of engagement metrics "makes the problem harder" — [CMX: Measure the value of contribution, not the value of community](https://www.cmxhub.com/blog/measure-the-value-of-contribution-not-the-value-of-community)
- Spinks, in his Product Hunt AMA, warned against measuring engagement without understanding how it leads to business impact and said to start by defining the business objective; the book frames SPACES as a way to set business and community goals that reinforce each other and are measurable — [Product Hunt AMA](https://www.producthunt.com/discussions/i-m-david-spinks-i-ve-fostered-communities-for-10-years-wrote-the-business-of-belonging-ama); [TechnologyAdvice summary](https://solutions.technologyadvice.com/?p=19314)
- CMX Community Industry Report 2021: "many of the most popular metrics measure community health, rather than business impact. Only 28% of community professionals surveyed indicate that they track new customers and 26% track customer retention"; the 2020 report flagged that tracked metrics "are often vanity measures" — [Top takeaways from the CMX report (2021)](https://grey-maroon-4599.squarespace.com/blog/2021/3/11/top-takeaways-from-the-cmx-community-industry-report); [CMX 2020 report](https://cmxhub.com/2020-community-industry-report/)
- CMX Community Industry Report 2024 (6th edition) headline: community teams are "small but scrappy"; 2025 edition: teams are leaner and budgets have taken a hit; 2026 (7th) edition: 93% of community professionals use AI tools, up from 81% — [CMX 2024](https://www.cmxhub.com/community-industry-report-2024); [CMX 2025](https://www.cmxhub.com/community-industry-report-2025); [CMX 2026](https://www.cmxhub.com/community-industry-report); PDF of 2025 edition hosted by Bevy: [2025 CMX Community Industry Report PDF](https://start.bevy.com/hubfs/2025%20CMX%20Community%20Industry%20Report-1.pdf?hsLang=en)
- Australian Community Managers' State of Community Management 2024: 45% of professionals track ROI, but most "are struggling to quantify community value in dollar terms" — [Michelle Goodall summary of ACM 2024](https://michelle-goodall.squarespace.com/insights/resource-state-of-community-management-2024-acm)
- Orbit Model (Josh Dzielak, Patrick Woods, orbit.love): two axes, *love* (commitment/involvement) and *reach* (how far a member's actions extend beyond the community); *gravity* = love x reach; "A high gravity community is one that excels at attracting and retaining members"; the repo changelog confirms sections with "calculations and example tables for each metric" — [orbit-love/orbit-model GitHub](https://github.com/orbit-love/orbit-model); [Chabot blog summary](https://chris-chabot-blog.pages.dev/almanac/03-frameworks/orbit-model/)
- Orbit levels: Level 1 Advocates, Level 2 Contributors, Level 3 Participants, Level 4 Explorers/Observers; Orbit later proposed renaming to Building / Contributing / Participating / Exploring and said per-community percentages are configurable; guidance is to judge *balance between adjacent levels* (e.g., "10 Advocates but only 5 Contributors" is a lost mentoring opportunity) rather than hit fixed shares — [Orbit blog: Orbit Model upgrades](https://orbit.love/blog/orbit-model-upgrades-for-proactive-community-building); [orbit-model.vercel.app: balancing orbit levels](https://orbit-model.vercel.app/resources/balancing-orbit-levels); [DevRel talk on love and reach](https://developerrelations.com/talks/how-to-measure-developer-love-and-reach/)
- One secondary source estimates Advocates at ~1-2% of a community (unofficial; Orbit itself does not publish fixed percentages) — [Chabot blog](https://chris-chabot-blog.pages.dev/almanac/03-frameworks/orbit-model/)
- Commsor's "Community Metrics: What to Track and Why" breaks metrics into three buckets (excerpt did not name them) — [Commsor blog](https://commsor.com/post/community-metrics). Common Room separates *community health* metrics from *business impact* metrics and places attribution (e.g., community-attributed revenue, % of revenue from community-engaged organizations) under business impact — [Common Room](https://www.commonroom.io/blog/what-your-community-metrics-and-analytics-are-telling-you). A Monetizely guide attributes to a Commsor Community Industry Report the claim that mature communities attribute 27% of new customer acquisition to community influence (unverified against the original) — [Monetizely](https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/how-to-measure-community-led-growth-metrics-a-guide-for-saas-executives)
- Millington/FeverBee on 90-9-1: it "crossed over from a Wikipedia observation to a rule defining participation in any community"; no convincing psychological explanation; "doesn't apply for smaller communities"; figures range "from 0.001 to 20%"; counting inactive registered accounts distorts the picture; the useful question is how activity varies among people who *do* participate — [FeverBee: How to treat the 90-9-1 theory](https://www.feverbee.com/how-to-treat-the-90-9-1-theory/); [FeverBee: A systematic destruction](https://www.feverbee.com/a-systematic-destruction); [FeverBee ?p=10236](https://www.feverbee.com/?p=10236)
- CMX's own Slack community data (three quarters): "super active" (posted in a channel or DM) = 2%, "active" (read at least one channel or DM) = 5%, passive = 93% — i.e., ~7% of members read or post in a quarter — [CMX: The 1-9-90 theory of engagement](https://cmxhub.com/1-9-90-theory-of-engagement)
- Higher Logic (vendor) data: up to 23% of members actively create new content, while the ~9% "contributor" share was roughly consistent with the rule — [Higher Logic](https://www.higherlogic.com/?p=3130). CustomerThink (2011): all but one community studied had more commenters than 9%; the rule implicitly treats every registered user as active — [CustomerThink](https://customerthink.com/is_the_90_9_1_rule_for_online_community_engagement_dead_data/). Khoros: "not a hard and fast rule. Not even the 9-1 part of it" — [Khoros](https://community.khoros.com/blog/release-notes/the-90-9-1-rule-in-reality/5463)
- Airbyte's open-source Slack community (2021, early stage): 450 of 1,030 members active weekly (~45%); authors describe it as a deliberately high-touch "user success" program — [Airbyte on DEV](https://dev.to/airbytehq/how-user-success-helps-us-become-the-most-active-slack-community-4j4b)

### Inferences
- For a 252-activated-member platform, the right denominator for engagement shares is *activated/vetted members*, not all 1,900 accounts; both Millington and CMX say counting dormant registrations is what makes 90-9-1 look true.
- SPACES is best used here as a goal taxonomy: LaFamilia's community value maps mostly to **Acquisition** (referrals of vetted founders/investors), **Engagement** (intros, replies) and **Success** (founders getting meetings/capital). Pick north-star inside one of those, not "engagement" in general.
- Orbit levels translate directly to a founder/investor platform: Advocates = members who refer and host; Contributors = members who post offers/asks or accept intros; Participants = members who reply/attend; Explorers = activated but silent. Tracking the *ratio between adjacent levels* is a usable health metric at n=252 where percentages are noisy.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve the 2022-2025 CMX reports' engagement/ROI percentage tables (site blocked); only 2020/2021 numbers and 2024-2026 headlines surfaced.
- Orbit Model's official example percentages per level and its love/reach formulas (in the repo's pages/ folder) were not readable from the fetched README.
- Commsor's three "buckets" were not named in any accessible excerpt.
- No source gave engagement benchmarks specifically for *paid or vetted professional* communities (vs. open Slack/forum communities).

## KQ2. Marketplace and network metrics: liquidity, match rate, time-to-first-match, fill rate, supply/demand ratio, response rate/time

### Takeaway
a16z, Lenny Rachitsky and Point Nine converge on replacing "liquidity" with two measurable signals, fill/match rate and time-to-match, measured per segment (geo/category, or here: stage/sector/investor type). Platform-published response-rate thresholds are rare; the only concrete numbers found are Thumbtack's Platinum-status requirement (per pros) and a vendor benchmark of ~20-25% investor opt-in to intro requests.

### Cited Findings
- a16z "13 Metrics for Marketplace Companies" (Jeff Jordan, Li Jin, D'Arcy Coolican, Andrew Chen; Feb 21 2020) is framed as a primer; it defines *match rate* (also called utilization or success rate) and *time to match* ("or inventory turnover, or days to turn" — how long supply and demand take to match); it recommends tracking metrics by geography or category segment "especially if your network effects are local" — [a16z: 13 metrics](https://a16z.com/13-metrics-for-marketplace-companies/); reprinted in [a16z Marketplace 100 2021 PDF](https://info.a16z.com/rs/382-JZB-798/images/marketplace-100-2021.pdf); third-party summary [The Marketplace Guide](https://themarketplaceguide.pages.dev/articles/the-13-metrics-that-actually-reveal-if-your-marketplace-is-working-and-what-each-one-signals/)
- Companion a16z essays: "16 ways to measure network effects" and "Marketplace engagement" — [a16z: 16 ways](https://a16z.com/16-ways-to-measure-network-effects/); [a16z: marketplace engagement](https://a16z.com/2020/02/20/marketplace-engagement/)
- Lenny Rachitsky, "The most important marketplace metrics": *fill rate* = "Percentage of intentful sessions that end up converting"; in his "Why marketplaces fail" framing a marketplace fails if it can't match reliably (fill rate) or quickly (time-to-match) — [Lenny's Newsletter: most important marketplace metrics](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-most-important-marketplace-metrics); Benjamin Lauzier (ex-Lyft/Thumbtack) interview describes fill rate as the percentage of demand that successfully translates into transactions — [Lenny's Newsletter: How marketplaces win (Lauzier)](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-marketplaces-win-benjamin-lauzier)
- Point Nine ("WTF is marketplace liquidity?"): measure *search-to-fill* and *utilization* "on a granular per category and per geo basis"; time-to-fill matters most "where the platform picks the match for the buyer" — [Point Nine](https://writing.pointnine.com/p/wtf-is-marketplace-liquidity)
- Rework guide: replace the ambiguous term "liquidity" with specific signals like fill rate and time-to-match — [Rework: What is marketplace liquidity](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/marketplace-growth/marketplace-liquidity)
- Upwork shows an "Avg. Response Time" responsiveness tag (bands e.g. 0-4h), covering pre-contract and in-contract messages, updated weekly; feature was in testing at time of doc — [Upwork: responsiveness tag](https://support.upwork.com/hc/en-us/articles/43854361755411-What-s-the-responsiveness-tag); [Upwork: find responsive freelancers](https://support.upwork.com/hc/en-us/articles/43854584840595-How-do-I-find-responsive-freelancers)
- Thumbtack pros report (forum, anecdotal) that Platinum status requires "75% reply within an hour"; response time "has always been a factor in reaching Top Pro status"; high-response-rate pros get better lead distribution — [Thumbtack community thread](https://community.thumbtack.com/discussion/832/problem-with-pro-tracking-of-my-response-times); [Thumbtack response time thread](https://community.thumbtack.com/discussion/comment/6121/); [FieldPie Thumbtack guide](https://www.fieldpie.com/?p=12899)
- Gigradar (vendor, "directional ranges") defines Upwork reply rate as "% of proposals that receive any buyer response within 7 days" — [Gigradar benchmarks](https://gigradar.io/blog/benchmark-overview-reply-shortlist-win-rates-by-category-budget)
- Founder-investor intro requests: Metal.so (vendor platform data) reports a platform median of ~25% investor response and cites an industry range of 20-25% median opt-in to introduction requests — [Metal.so intro response benchmark](https://www.metal.so/data-desk/intro-response-benchmark). Boomerang (vendor, no citation) claims 60-80% acceptance for well-run double opt-in intros; this conflicts with the 20-25% figure — [Boomerang glossary](https://getboomerang.ai/glossaries/double-opt-in-introductions)
- Double opt-in intro rationale: investors "receive many more introduction requests than they can take"; the connector's reputation rides on each forward; a missing/weak blurb causes the investor to skip the intro — [Happenstance](https://happenstance.ai/how-to/double-opt-in-intro); [Draftboard](https://www.draftboard.com/blog/how-investors-make-warm-introductions); [Chris Neumann: Why I won't promise you an intro](https://chrisneumann.squarespace.com/archives/why-i-wont-promise-you-an-intro)

### Inferences
- Usable formulas for LaFamilia: **Match/fill rate** = intros (or asks) that result in an accepted connection / intros requested; **Time-to-first-match** = median days from activation to first accepted intro or reply; **Supply/demand ratio** = active investors (responded in last 30d) / active founders seeking capital; **Response rate** = share of intro requests answered (accept or decline) within 7 days; **Response time** = median hours to first reply. All should be cut by segment (stage, sector, geography, investor type), per a16z/Point Nine.
- At ~252 activated members, a 20-25% investor opt-in median (Metal.so) implies a founder sending 10 requests gets ~2-3 opt-ins; a curated, pre-vetted platform should aim to beat that, and the gap vs. the 60-80% vendor claim is itself a candidate experiment outcome.
- Thumbtack's "75% within an hour" is a consumer-services SLA and will not transfer to investors; a 7-day response window (Gigradar-style) is more realistic for investor-side guardrails.

### Gaps
- Full a16z list of 13 metrics with definitions could not be retrieved (host blocked); only match rate and time-to-match were confirmed from excerpts.
- No published, independent benchmarks for fill rate or time-to-match for *professional networking or founder-investor* platforms; all marketplace numbers found are consumer services (Thumbtack) or freelance (Upwork) and may not transfer.
- Airbnb response-rate/time thresholds were not surfaced in this research.

## KQ3. Viral and referral metrics: K-factor, realistic ranges for invite-only products, referral acceptance rates, viral cycle time

### Takeaway
K = invites per user x conversion rate of invites; sustained K > 1 is essentially mythical (Vohra: "no feature or product has ever really had a viral loop of greater than one for any meaningful period of time"), and Skok argues cycle time matters more than K. Celebrated cases were sub-1: Dropbox ~0.7 (secondary citation). Vendor referral-funnel benchmarks for B2B SaaS put share rates at ~5-30% and referred-visitor conversion at ~10-25%, with definitions varying so much that only within-platform trends are reliable.

### Cited Findings
- Skok's formula: K = invites sent per user x % who convert; K must exceed 1 for self-sustaining growth; viral cycle time (how fast the loop repeats) can matter more than K itself — [Skok, Lessons learnt - viral marketing](https://www.forentrepreneurs.com/lessons-learnt-viral-marketing/) (as excerpted via [echai summary](https://echai.ventures/startingup/r/lessons-learned-viral-marketing--f-r144)); First Round glossary definition — [First Round: K-factor](https://review.firstround.com/glossary/k-factor-virality/)
- Dropbox and WhatsApp early K-factors reported as 0.7 and 0.4 respectively (Wikipedia, secondary; not verified against Skok/Dropbox) — [Wikipedia: K-factor (marketing)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-factor_(marketing)). Intercom: Dropbox "benefitted heavily from social distribution despite not going viral" — [Intercom: viral loops](https://www.intercom.com/blog/viral-loops/)
- Dropbox referral program (launched 2008; 500MB to referrer and referee) is reported to have "permanently increased signups by 60%" with "more than 2.8 million direct referral invites sent the first 18 months" (secondary blogs); another blog says 2 million invites in one month; the S-1 lists word of mouth, in-product referrals and sharing together as the growth engine — [Viral Loops: Dropbox](https://viral-loops.com/blog/dropbox-grew-3900-simple-referral-program/?authuser=0); [Hackernoon: Dropbox 3900%](https://hackernoon.com/how-dropbox-grew-3900percent-using-referral-marketing-0qp3u7s); [Referral Rock](https://referralrock.com/blog/?p=31171); [Business Insider India](https://businessinsider.in/strategy/news/heres-how-dropbox-billionaire-drew-houston-hacked-viral-marketing-and-user-retention-to-launch-a-4-billion-company/articleshow/72232518.cms)
- A separate secondary source says that initially 60% of people who signed up via referral did not go on to use Dropbox (a retention problem; not the same 60%) — [Nira: Dropbox history](https://nira.com/dropbox-history/)
- Rahul Vohra (Superhuman) in an a16z conversation: "no feature or product has ever really had a viral loop of greater than one for any meaningful period of time" — [a16z ?p=17257](https://a16z.com/?p=17257). Kwokchain: Superhuman's invite-only access let it "choose their growth rate, pre-qualify their sales leads, and drum up even more organic interest"; referring someone "was itself a strong indicator of interest"; an automated survey screened referred users before 1:1 onboarding; loop is driven by social capital, not utility — [Kwokchain: Notes on Superhuman's acquisition loops](https://kwokchain.com/?p=345); [Lenny's Newsletter: Superhuman's secret](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/superhumans-secret-to-success-rahul-vohra)
- Three invite-loop levers (Slack analysis): % of users who share, invites per sharing user, conversion from viewed share link to sign-up (invite acceptance rate) — [UX Collective: Slack's invite flow](https://uxdesign.cc/what-slacks-invite-flow-teaches-us-about-virality-6e1b42e81572)
- Vendor referral benchmarks (B2B SaaS; vendors have an interest): Cello: participation rate 5-15%, share rate 10-30%, click-to-conversion 15-25% — [Cello](https://cello.so/?p=14760); Prefinery: share rate 25-35% at growth stage, SaaS referral rate 4.75% — [Prefinery](https://prefinery.com/blog/10-key-referral-program-metrics-to-track-2025); Extole (400 programs): B2B share rate 4.8%, conversion defined as conversions / share clicks — [Extole: 4 key funnel metrics](https://www.extole.com/blog/4-key-funnel-metrics-from-400-referral-programs/); Fungies: referral conversion median 10-15%, top quartile 20%+, "most SaaS companies see 2-5%" — [Fungies](https://fungies.io/saas-referral-program-guide.md)
- Sub-1 K still compounds but plateaus without other acquisition; sustained K>1 "mostly limited to products where inviting others is core to the value" — [echai: is K>1 realistic](https://echai.ventures/startingup/retention-referral/what-s-a-viral-coefficient-and-is-chasing-a-k-factor-above-1-realistic-for-my-kind-of-product); [Branch: app virality](https://www.branch.io/resources/blog/dissecting-app-virality-from-app-k-factor-to-how-to-use-emotion-to-drive-more-organic-app-downloads/)

### Inferences
- For an invite-only platform the operative decomposition is: K = (share of activated members who invite) x (invites per inviter) x (invite acceptance rate) x (vetting pass rate). The last factor is unique to curated growth and is also the quality guardrail (see KQ6).
- With 252 activated members, K is measurable but noisy: e.g., 25% inviting x 2 invites x 40% acceptance x 70% vetting pass = K ≈ 0.14 (~35 new vetted members per cycle). A realistic target band for a curated professional product is K 0.2-0.5 with cycle time measured in weeks; anything near 1 would contradict Vohra/Skok and should trigger a Twyman's-law check (see KQ5).
- Dropbox's reported "60% of referred sign-ups never used the product" is the most relevant Dropbox number here: it argues for measuring *activated referred members*, not referred sign-ups, which also matches LaFamilia's 1,900 vs 252 gap.

### Gaps
- No published invite acceptance rate for Superhuman, Clubhouse or other invite-only products was found.
- The Dropbox 60%/2.8M figures are secondhand (growth-marketing blogs) and were not confirmed against Houston's talks.
- No benchmark found for referral acceptance rates specifically among investors or senior professionals; all referral benchmarks are consumer or SaaS.

## KQ4. Activation and retention: choosing an activation metric; retention benchmarks for professional networks and communities

### Takeaway
Reforge/Lenny guidance is to define activation as a core value action within a time window that *causally* predicts retention, split into Setup -> Aha -> Habit, and to measure activation as a ratio of setup-completers. Published retention benchmarks are almost all consumer or SaaS: Lenny/Winters' survey gives 6-month user retention "good/great" bars (consumer social ~25%/45%, consumer SaaS ~40%/70%, SMB SaaS ~60%/80%), Mixpanel reports week-1 retention falling from 50% to 28% in 2023, and DAU/MAU heuristics range 10-50%. None are for vetted professional networks.

### Cited Findings
- Reforge (via summaries; original blocked): describe the aha moment qualitatively and quantitatively ("a core action within a set time window"); break activation into Setup, Aha, Habit "instead of one 'X in Y days' metric"; "More users taking the key action only matters if those users come back"; measure activation as users who hit aha / users who completed setup — [echai: how to measure activation](https://echai.ventures/startingup/growth-metrics/how-do-i-measure-activation-and-where-should-the-activation-moment-actually-sit-in-my-onboarding); [echai: define your aha moment](https://echai.ventures/startingup/r/define-your-aha-moment--bc-r209)
- Lenny, "How to determine your activation metric": list at least five candidate behaviors, pick the one with greatest variance in retention between users who do/don't do it; stricter test: experimentally raising the share who hit the moment also raises retention ("a real aha moment metric needs to pass a causal test"); avoid vanity gates like sign-up ("if a user did this and only this, would they still retain?") — [Lenny's Newsletter: activation metric](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-determine-your-activation); [UXCam: wrong aha moment](https://uxcam.com/blog/why-most-teams-have-the-wrong-aha-moment/); [PostHog: WTF is activation](https://posthog.com/newsletter/wtf-is-activation.md)
- Lenny Rachitsky & Casey Winters, "What is good retention?" (survey of ~20 growth practitioners), 6-month *user* retention good/great: consumer social ~25%/~45%; consumer transactional ~30%/~50%; consumer SaaS ~40%/~70%; SMB/mid-market SaaS ~60%/~80%; enterprise SaaS ~70%/~90%. Net revenue retention good/great: consumer SaaS ~55%/~80%; bottom-up SaaS ~100%/~120%; VSB land-and-expand ~80%/~100%; enterprise ~110%/~130%. Lower is OK if CAC and marginal costs are low, you're not building venture-scale, or you're just starting out; "startups rarely increase retention significantly" — [Lenny's Newsletter: What is good retention (issue 29)](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-good-retention-issue-29); [Casey Winters' companion post](https://www.caseyaccidental.com/p/what-is-good-retention-an-exhaustive-benchmark-study-with-lenny-rachitsky); follow-up [What is good retention and how to improve it](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-good-retention-and-how-to-improve-it)
- Brian Balfour: most published benchmarks are averages; build a bottoms-up model instead — [Balfour: retention benchmarks](https://brianbalfour.com/quick-takes/retention-benchmarks)
- Mixpanel 2024 Benchmarks Report (as cited by Mixpanel community): average week-1 retention across industries fell from 50% to 28% in 2023 — [Mixpanel 2024 Benchmarks Report PDF](https://mixpanel.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/2024_Benchmarks_Report.pdf); [Mixpanel community answer](https://community.mixpanel.com/x/ask-ai/s727rrc6c6ng/understanding-mixpanel-retention-rates-and-mau-ben). Mixpanel 2019 Product Benchmarks (1.3B users): 8-week retention above 25% "elite" for media/finance, above 35% elite for SaaS/e-commerce — [Mixpanel 2019 blog](https://mixpanel.com/blog/2019-product-benchmarks-report-mixpanel). Mixpanel mobile data (secondhand, undated): only Games and Messaging hit Fred Wilson's 30% one-month retention bar (31%) — [Mixpanel: what's a good retention rate](https://mixpanel.com/blog/whats-a-good-retention-rate/)
- Amplitude: "there aren't any super well-established benchmarks" for day-30 retention; most published stats used rolling rather than day-N retention; stickiness = DAU/MAU x 100 and "doesn't tell you anything about why"; Amplitude's interactive benchmark tool segments by industry/region/size and shows month-1 retention relative to all companies — [Amplitude: which app to benchmark against](https://amplitude.com/blog/whats-the-one-app-you-should-measure-your-retention-against); [Amplitude product benchmarks guide](https://www.amplitude.com/blog/product-benchmarks-guide); [Amplitude engagement benchmarks](https://amplitude.com/benchmarks/engagement)

## KQ5. Experimentation with small samples: MDE, sequential testing, Bayesian A/B, holdouts, pre-registration, and when to go qualitative

### Takeaway
With ~250 activated members, classical A/B tests can only detect very large effects (own calculation: with 126 per arm you can detect a 30%->47% move, not 30%->36%); Kohavi's work shows typical real lifts are under 1-3% and underpowered "wins" overstate effects ~4-5x. Sequential and Bayesian methods help with stopping rules but do not create sample size; practitioners therefore recommend qualitative discovery (Torres: weekly interviews + assumption tests), before/after with a small holdout for cumulative impact, and pre-registered decision rules.

### Cited Findings
- Evan Miller's sample-size calculator: MDE is "the smallest effect that will be detected (1-β)% of the time"; inputs are baseline conversion rate, MDE, power and significance; it uses the classical fixed-horizon two-proportion formula — [Evan Miller: sample size](https://www.evanmiller.org/ab-testing/sample-size.html); [Optibase (notes it uses the same fixed-horizon formula)](https://optibase.io/ab-test-sample-size-calculator); formula components (p1, p2 = p1 + absolute MDE, z-scores for α and power) — [AB Tasty](https://www.abtasty.com/blog/sample-size-calculation/); [arXiv 2305.16459 (per-arm formula with pooled proportion)](https://pdf.arxiv.org/pdf/2305.16459)
- Worked vendor examples: Optimizely — baseline 20% with 10% relative MDE detects moves outside 18-22% — [Optimizely calculator](https://www.optimizely.com/sample-size-calculator); HubSpot — 2% baseline, 20% relative lift needs ~20,000 per group — [HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/email-a-b-test-sample-size-testing-time?authuser=0)
- Kohavi, Tang & Xu, *Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments* (Cambridge, 2020): draws on companies running >20,000 experiments/year — [Cambridge excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811087/24265/excerpt/9781108724265_excerpt.pdf); Kohavi's practitioner paper "Online Controlled Experiments at Large Scale" — [Kohavi et al. KDD](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse454/13wi/papers/p959-kohavi.pdf)
- Kohavi on Twyman's Law ("Any figure that looks interesting or different is usually wrong"): at Microsoft and Airbnb "probably 9 out of 10 times we found an issue that invalidated the result"; real lifts are "typically under one percent, rarely over two or three percent"; sample ratio mismatch (e.g., 44:56 from a 50:50 design) means the experiment "is deemed not trustworthy" and is a standard guardrail — [Kohavi et al. 2026 (rounded shapes replication)](https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/research/published/KohaviEtAlMarch2026.pdf); [arXiv 2512.24521](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.24521); [Wikipedia: Sample ratio mismatch](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Sample_ratio_mismatch); Airbnb/Kohavi "A/B testing intuition busters" — [Airbnb tech PDF](https://airbnb.tech/wp-content/uploads/sites/19/2025/10/ABTestingIntuitionBusters.pdf)
- Kohavi critique of a published 75% CTR lift: estimated power was 7.3%, not 80%; a significant result from such a test exaggerates the lift "by a factor of 4.6" (23% observed vs 5% true) — [Kohavi: When not to trust a published A/B test](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1IDcLEr58QoQU2VJPc-TefLMLegdQMnh3/mobilebasic)
- Peeking: frequentist tests assume one look at a predetermined sample; repeated checks are multiple tests; remedies are set MDE, compute n at 80% power, run to n without early exits; sequential testing is "a middle path"; Bayesian testing "does not solve the peeking problem" — [Kameleoon: data accuracy pitfalls](https://kameleoon.com/blog/data-accuracy-pitfalls-ab-testing); [Towards Data Science: most A/B tests are lying](https://towardsdatascience.com/why-most-a-b-tests-are-lying-to-you/); [Kameleoon: Kohavi interview](https://www.kameleoon.com/enronny-kohavi-getting-results-you-trust)
- Sequential testing for low volume: captures upside of early stopping on *large* effects, but on low-traffic pages tests "often never trigger efficacy boundaries and run to the maximum sample size anyway. Low traffic doesn't disappear with a better method" — [Towards Data Science: sequential testing for low-volume tests](https://towardsdatascience.com/sequential-testing-the-secret-sauce-for-low-volume-a-b-tests-fe62bdf9627b/); [Convert: sequential testing](https://www.convert.com/glossary/sequential-testing/); [Kameleoon: role of sequential testing](https://kameleoon.com/blog/role-sequential-testing-ab-testing)
- Bayesian A/B: informative priors can reduce required n but "defining priors accurately is genuinely difficult"; with non-informative priors required sample sizes are "very similar" to frequentist — [Optimizely: choosing a stats engine](https://www.optimizely.com/field-notes/articles/how-to-choose-the-right-stats-engine); [Statsig: Bayesian A/B](https://statsig.com/glossary/bayesian-ab-test)
- Low-traffic strategies: ship a series of best-practice/qualitative-research-driven improvements and measure cumulative impact with a holdout; supplement with qualitative methods that need smaller samples (surveys, session recordings, interviews) — [Atticus Li: low-traffic A/B strategies](https://www.atticusli.com/blog/posts/low-traffic-ab-tests-small-sample-strategies/); [VWO: low-traffic sites](https://vwo.com/blog/ab-split-testing-low-traffic-sites/)
- Holdouts: a small slice (typically up to 5%, Statsig/Optimizely) kept away from all new features for a finite period; must be created before the experiments it covers (LaunchDarkly); Pinterest held out 1% for over a year to confirm long-term effects; CXL warns holdouts need "massive amounts of traffic" to be statistically meaningful — [GrowthBook: holdouts](https://www.growthbook.io/blog/holdouts-in-growthbook); [Statsig: holdout group](https://statsig.com/glossary/holdout-group); [Optimizely: global holdouts](https://docs.developers.optimizely.com/feature-experimentation/docs/global-holdouts); [LaunchDarkly: holdouts](https://launchdarkly.com/docs/guides/experimentation/holdouts); [Pinterest Engineering](https://medium.com/pinterest-engineering/how-holdout-groups-drive-sustainable-growth-35a4786c3801); [CXL: hold-out groups](https://cxl.com/blog/hold-out-groups/)
- Teresa Torres, continuous discovery: "At a minimum, weekly touch points with customers" by the team building the product; weekly routine of interviews, assumption tests (lightweight checks of key beliefs before committing build resources) and synthesis; the product trio (PM, designer, engineer) all take part; the opportunity solution tree is updated weekly — [Product Talk glossary](https://www.producttalk.org/glossary-discovery-continuous-discovery/); [UserTesting podcast with Torres](https://www.usertesting.com/resources/podcast/continuous-discovery-customer-centric-product-development); [Product Talk: Y Oslo 2024, "something is better than nothing"](https://www.producttalk.org/2024/12/y-oslo-2024/); [Lenny's Podcast summary](https://podpage.com/lennys-podcast/blog/summary-build-better-products-with-continuous-product-discovery-teresa-torres)

### Inferences
- Own calculation (two-sided α=0.05, power 0.80, two-proportion z-test; not from a source): per-arm n needed — 10%->12%: 3,841; 10%->15%: 686; 10%->20%: 199; 20%->30%: 293; 30%->40%: 356; 30%->45%: 162; 50%->65%: 169; 5%->10%: 434; 13%->20%: 440. Conversely, splitting the 252 activated members 50/50 (126 per arm) only detects: 10%->23%, 20%->36%, 30%->47%, 50%->67%. Splitting all 1,900 accounts (950 per arm) detects 10%->14%, 20%->25%, 30%->36%, 50%->56%.
- Practical implication: of the 10 planned experiments, only those run on the *whole 1,900-account funnel* (e.g., activation emails, onboarding flow, invite prompts to dormant accounts) can be true randomized A/B tests with realistic MDEs (~4-6 points absolute). Experiments on the 252 activated members (intro formats, matching rules, event formats) should be run as (a) pre-registered before/after with a 10-20% holdout rather than 5% (CXL's warning applies: 5% of 252 is 13 people), (b) within-member crossover designs (each member experiences both variants in alternating weeks, which roughly halves variance for behavioral rates), or (c) qualitative/assumption tests per Torres with pre-set success thresholds.
- Pre-registration at this scale is mainly a discipline against Twyman's-law false positives: write hypothesis, primary metric, MDE, n and stop date before launch; declare any lift larger than ~2x the pre-registered MDE as "suspicious, re-check instrumentation and SRM first" (Kohavi).
- Sequential tests are still worth using on the 1,900-account funnel because they let you stop early if an effect is large, which is the only kind of effect detectable here; Bayesian posteriors are useful for *communicating* uncertainty to a founder audience but not for shrinking n.

### Gaps
- Could not read Kohavi's book chapters directly; small-sample pitfalls are from his papers and secondary interviews.
- Evan Miller's own page text (and his sequential-sampling and Bayesian posts) could not be fetched; MDE definition is from excerpt.
- No source gave a formal rule for "when to stop A/B testing and go qualitative"; the threshold stated above (if required n exceeds what you can reach in a 2-6 week window, per Kameleoon) is the closest found.

## KQ6. Guardrail metrics for curated growth: invited-member quality, spam complaints, investor fatigue, high-value churn; how On Deck, Chief, Hampton, YC guard quality

### Takeaway
Curated communities publish *selectivity* rather than guardrail dashboards: Hampton ~8% acceptance with member veto; Chief grew to 10,000 members with a 40,000-60,000 waitlist before moving to application-based review; On Deck's acceptance is unpublished (third-party estimates 10-30%). The concrete guardrail numbers that transfer are investor response/opt-in (~20-25% median), SRM/instrumentation checks, and churn of high-value members, which no source benchmarks for founder networks.

### Cited Findings
- Hampton (Sam Parr): "Every member is vetted. Only ~8% are accepted"; criteria: founder/CEO/owner with $3M+ revenue, $3M+ raised, or $10M+ exit; admission includes community-wide review where "any member can raise an objection before someone is admitted", plus co-founder sign-off; Core Groups of eight founders meeting monthly, placement within 90 days; seats per city capped per cycle; bootstrapped so "growth decisions prioritize member quality over headcount" — [Hampton](https://joinhampton.com/); [Hampton FAQ](https://joinhampton.com/faq); [Hampton moderators](https://joinhampton.com/moderators); [Asora on Hampton](https://www.asora.com/family-office-networks/hampton); [Hampton blog: founder peer group in 7 steps](https://joinhampton.com/blog/how-to-choose-a-founder-peer-group-in-7-steps-2026); [TechCrunch on Hampton](https://techcrunch.com/2023/03/29/hampton-is-techs-new-membership-community-for-chief-executive-officers); third-party caveat that chapter quality "hinges on the specific person leading your cohort" — [Hampton vs Vistage](https://joinhampton.com/blog/hampton-vs-vistage-which-peer-group-is-better-for-founders)
- Chief: launched NYC 2019; by Jan 2022 10,000+ members in seven cities with a 40,000 waitlist; 2023 reporting put the waitlist at 60,000 and described growing pains; Chief has since "moved to an application-based model" with a 30-minute qualifying call; nominated candidates "skip past our waitlist" — [BusinessWire: Chief national expansion](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20220111005981/en/Chief-the-Private-Network-for-Women-Leaders-Announces-National-Expansion); [Fortune 2023: Chief growing pains](https://fortune.com/2023/03/20/chief-womens-network-startup-members-waitlist-valuation-growing-pains/); [Chief FAQ](https://www.chief.com/faq); [Chief nominations](https://chief.com/nominations-web-accept)
- On Deck: describes itself as "a curated community designed to increase your odds of building a successful venture-backed company"; no published acceptance rate; a competitor's review estimates 10-30% for popular tracks (unverified) — [On Deck Fellowships (Medium)](https://medium.com/@beondeck/the-next-chapter-on-deck-fellowships-b42f32942543); [Elev-X review of On Deck](https://elev-x.com/news-insights/article-on-deck-fellowship-review/); [Hive Index: On Deck](https://thehiveindex.com/communities/on-deck/)
- Superhuman used invite-only access to "choose their growth rate, pre-qualify their sales leads", screened referred users with an automated survey before 1:1 onboarding — [Kwokchain](https://kwokchain.com/?p=345)
- Investor-side fatigue signals: investors "receive many more introduction requests than they can take"; Metal.so median investor response ~25%, industry 20-25% opt-in — [Happenstance](https://happenstance.ai/how-to/double-opt-in-intro); [Metal.so](https://www.metal.so/data-desk/intro-response-benchmark)
- Thumbtack pros report contact/response rates as the metric the platform polices (Platinum requires ~75% reply within an hour; one pro saw response rate drop 16% after raising prices) — [Thumbtack community](https://community.thumbtack.com/discussion/832/problem-with-pro-tracking-of-my-response-times); [Thumbtack pricing thread](https://community.thumbtack.com/discussion/comment/5995/)
- Experiment-level guardrails from Kohavi: sample ratio mismatch check, Twyman's law review, power check before trusting a result — [Kohavi et al. 2026](https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/research/published/KohaviEtAlMarch2026.pdf); [Wikipedia: SRM](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Sample_ratio_mismatch)
- Spinks: community value = member contribution; measuring engagement alone is a trap — [CMX: value of contribution](https://www.cmxhub.com/blog/measure-the-value-of-contribution-not-the-value-of-community)

### Inferences
- A guardrail set for LaFamilia that can be computed at n=252 (definitions are the author's synthesis of the sources above): (1) **Vetting pass rate of referred applicants** (Hampton-style; a falling pass rate = inviters are reaching outside the target), (2) **Investor response rate within 7 days** and **declines-to-accepts ratio** per investor (fatigue signal if an investor's response rate drops >10 points month over month; Metal.so's 20-25% is the floor to beat), (3) **Intro requests per investor per week** capped (Happenstance/Draftboard rationale), (4) **Spam/abuse reports per 100 active members** and **unsubscribe rate on digests**, (5) **90-day retention of top-decile members** (Orbit Advocates/Contributors) tracked as a separate cohort because 25 people churning is invisible in aggregate but is the whole supply side, (6) **Member veto/objection count** if a Hampton-style community review is adopted, (7) SRM and instrumentation checks on every experiment.
- Chief's trajectory (10k members, 60k waitlist, then "growing pains" and a switch to application review) is the cautionary case: the waitlist itself became a quality and reputation problem; a curated platform should publish a decision SLA (Chief: outreach "within a few business days"; Hampton: core-group placement within 90 days) and treat time-to-decision as a guardrail.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies churn of high-value members, spam-complaint rates, or investor-fatigue thresholds for founder/investor networks; the numbers above are either consumer marketplaces (Thumbtack) or one vendor's intro data (Metal.so).
- YC's internal community (Bookface) quality metrics were not found in accessible sources.
- On Deck's and Chief's acceptance rates are unpublished.

## KQ7. Engagement benchmarks for professional networks or founder communities (weekly active share, posting share, reply rates)

### Takeaway
No independent benchmark set exists for vetted professional/founder communities. The closest datapoints are CMX's own Slack community (2% post, 5% read, 93% passive per quarter), Higher Logic's association data (up to 23% creating content), Airbyte's early open-source Slack (45% weekly active), and consumer DAU/MAU heuristics (10-50%). Treat these as a wide range to position against, not targets.

### Cited Findings
- CMX Slack community, three quarters: 2% super active (posted), 5% active (read), 93% passive — [CMX 1-9-90 study](https://cmxhub.com/1-9-90-theory-of-engagement)
- Higher Logic (association/customer communities): up to 23% of members actively create new content; ~9% contributor share consistent with 90-9-1 — [Higher Logic](https://www.higherlogic.com/?p=3130); Higher Logic's 2024 Association Community Benchmark Report covers engagement trends, digest open rate and monthly discussion activity (figures not in excerpt) — [Higher Logic 2024 association benchmark](https://higherlogic.com/blog/2024-association-community-benchmark-report)
- Airbyte open-source Slack (2021): 450 of 1,030 members active weekly (~45%) under a deliberate high-touch program — [Airbyte on DEV](https://dev.to/airbytehq/how-user-success-helps-us-become-the-most-active-slack-community-4j4b)
- Slack's analytics definitions usable for a posting-share metric: "daily people posting: number of people who have posted at least one message over the current day", weekly = rolling seven-day window — [Slack analytics dashboard help](https://slack.com/help/articles/360057638533); example of plotting "daily members posting messages / daily active members as a percentage" — [NHS-R community metrics](https://nhs-r-community.github.io/metrics/slack-metrics.html)
- Millington: participation inequality exists, but the useful measure is variation among those who do participate, excluding dormant registrations — [FeverBee](https://www.feverbee.com/how-to-treat-the-90-9-1-theory/)
- Consumer DAU/MAU heuristics (do not transfer directly): Wilson 30% monthly / 10% daily of registered; Chen >20% good, 50%+ world class; a16z 25% "OK" — [AVC](https://avc.com/2011/07/301010/); [Andrew Chen](https://andrewchen.com/dau-mau-is-an-important-metric/); [a16z](https://a16z.com/do-you-have-lightning-in-a-bottle-how-to-benchmark-your-social-app/)
- Investor reply benchmark to intro requests: ~20-25% median opt-in (vendor data) — [Metal.so](https://www.metal.so/data-desk/intro-response-benchmark)

### Inferences
- Positioning LaFamilia's current numbers: with 252 activated of 1,900 (13%), the platform is already above CMX's 7% read-or-post share if "activated" means any read/post; the meaningful comparison is weekly active / activated. A vetted, job-to-be-done network should sit between CMX's Slack (7%) and Airbyte's hand-held 45%: a working hypothesis of 20-30% weekly active among activated members, 5-10% posting/requesting weekly, and >40% reply rate to intro requests (roughly 2x the 20-25% investor-intro median) is defensible as an *initial* target band but is an inference, not a benchmark.
- Because all available benchmarks differ in denominator (registered vs. activated), window (daily/weekly/quarterly) and action (read vs post), the report should recommend publishing LaFamilia's own definitions first (Slack-style: "posted at least one message/request in rolling 7 days") and benchmarking against its own trend.

### Gaps
- No weekly-active, posting-share or reply-rate benchmarks were found for LinkedIn-style professional networks, founder communities (YC, On Deck, Hampton), or investor platforms; sources are open Slack communities, associations and consumer apps.
- Higher Logic 2024 association figures and Common Room benchmark reports were not retrievable in detail.
- Several hosts needed for primary verification (cmxhub.com, feverbee.com, a16z.com, lennysnewsletter.com, evanmiller.org, mixpanel.com, amplitude.com, forentrepreneurs.com, kwokchain.com, metal.so, statsig.com, towardsdatascience.com, echai.ventures, orbit-model.joshed.io) must be allowed in the session's network settings for page-level confirmation.
