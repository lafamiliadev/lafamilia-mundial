## Q1. How does Chen define an atomic network and the hard side, and what did Tinder, Uber, Slack, Zoom, Clubhouse and LinkedIn do by hand to seed the hard side?

### Takeaway
Chen defines the atomic network as "the smallest network needed with enough density and stability to stand on its own" and says every network has an "easy side" and a "hard side" (the minority who do disproportionate work and are hard to attract and keep; for marketplaces usually supply). The documented seeding stories are all manual and local: Tinder bought its way into one USC sorority's party (~500 people, download required at the door), Uber ran each city as its own cold start with autonomous ops teams and large driver-referral bonuses, Clubhouse's first network was literally "Paul and Paul's friends" in the Bay Area, and LinkedIn's 13 insiders hand-invited 112 people on launch day.

### Inferences
- For a founder/investor network, Chen's framework puts investors (the side that is scarce, busy and does the "work" of taking intros) on the hard side; the 100 opted-in investors are the asset, and the atomic network is probably a handful of investors plus the 10-20 founders they would actually take a meeting with, not 1,900 accounts.
- Every documented seed was geographically or socially bounded (one campus, one city, one friend group, one founder's contact list). The analog is one sector/geography/stage slice of LaFamilia where density can be made real within weeks.
- Chen's selection-effect observation (money-motivated drivers referred well; discount-seeking riders did not) suggests letting investors, not founders, drive invitations if quality matters.

- Chen's exact wording for "hard side" and the "1/9/90" participation rule could not be confirmed; the chapter and book text were not retrievable.
- Slack's atomic-network size is contested (3 vs 5-10) across secondhand sources.
- No source found on what Zoom or Slack did "by hand" to seed; Chen's Slack material in the book (Tiny Speck's own team, friends' companies) was not retrievable.

## Q2. Documented activation metrics (7 friends/10 days, 2,000 messages, 30 follows, etc.), their provenance, and how to find your own "magic number" (correlation vs causation caveats)

### Takeaway
"7 friends in 10 days" (Facebook, Chamath Palihapitiya, Growth Hackers Conference, Oct 26 2012) and "~30 follows, ~10 following back" (Twitter, Josh Elman) are first-person operator claims; Slack's "2,000 messages / 93% retained" is widely attributed to Stewart Butterfield in First Round Review but only secondhand confirmations were retrievable; LinkedIn's and Dropbox's thresholds are folklore with no primary source found. Practitioners (Lenny Rachitsky, Mixpanel, Mode, Statsig, Reforge) agree the method is: regress early behaviours against retention, find the inflection, then run experiments, because "a good activation metric is causal for your retention, not just correlative."

### Cited Findings

**Facebook: 7 friends in 10 days (documented, first-person)**
- The Growth Hackers Conference took place Friday October 26, 2012 at the Quadrus Conference Center, Menlo Park; Chamath Palihapitiya keynoted on "how his team helped put Facebook on the path to one billion users." — [The Next Web, Oct 20 2012](https://thenextweb.com/news/growth-hacker-conference)
- TechCrunch published the talk video on January 13, 2013, noting the organizers "have yet to share the video publicly, but Biyani was kind enough to give us a peek." (So "2012 talk" is correct; "2013" refers to publication.) — [TechCrunch, Jan 13 2013](https://techcrunch.com/2013/01/13/chamath-palihapitiya-on-growth-hacking-and-how-to-create-a-sustainable-user-acquisition-engine)
- The team "compared cohorts of engaged and unengaged users. The pattern that emerged was that the engaged cohorts had hit at least 7 friends within 10 days of signing up." — [25iq, "A dozen lessons on growth"](https://25iq.com/2017/02/10/a-dozen-lessons-on-growth)
- Greylock's Andy Johns write-up gives "7 friends within 10 days" as the threshold for becoming a daily active user; a separate summary inverts it to "10 friends within 7 days" (an error that circulates). — [Greylock, "Building a Growth Model for Your Company"](https://news.greylock.com/building-a-growth-model-for-your-company-a7a82c55782e); [Medium, "Build a Growth Machine Like Andy Johns"](https://medium.com/mobile-growth/build-a-growth-machine-like-andy-johns-83c8dcc9b40a)
- Mode's analysis argues Facebook's aha moment "is a set of actions that separate customers who find value in your product from those who don't," and that if a candidate action were merely correlated "the correlation would start to break down" under testing. — [Mode, "Facebook's aha moment was simpler than you think"](https://mode.com/blog/facebook-aha-moment-simpler-than-you-think/)

**Twitter: ~30 follows (documented via Josh Elman interviews; numbers secondhand)**
- Analysis of highly retained users (visiting at least seven times a month) found they typically followed about 30 accounts, with 30 as a tipping point; the team also found activation depended on how many of those accounts followed back, which Elman put at "roughly 10." — [Mattermark, "How Josh Elman identified a growth driver at Twitter"](https://mattermark.com/putting-it-all-together-how-josh-elman-identified-a-growth-driver-at-twitter/); [Medium, "Reading Josh Elman on growth"](https://medium.com/growth-hacker/reading-josh-elman-on-growth-6d379a6c153d)
- Onboarding was rebuilt to get new users to 30 follows quickly, replacing a list of ~20 mostly random suggested accounts. — [Fahrenheit Marketing summary](https://www.fahrenheitmarketing.com/twitter-improved-user-retention-rates-with-counter-intuition); [Hacking Growth reader notes](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/34220175-hacking-growth/532128-daniel/3d3f1dbb-345a-4f2e-aa36-063ed33de3c7)
- Elman's broader frame is "deliberate actions" taken by users as the signal of real engagement. — [Medium, "Path to Success: Create Deliberate Actions w/ Josh Elman"](https://medium.com/meet-the-operators/path-to-success-create-deliberate-actions-969ccb6702a2)

**Slack: 2,000 messages (widely attributed to Butterfield; primary text not retrievable)**
- The First Round Review article "From 0 to $1B: Slack's Founder Shares Their Epic Launch Strategy" is the usual primary citation. The body could not be fetched in this session. — [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/from-0-to-1b-slacks-founder-shares-their-epic-launch-strategy/)
- Secondhand confirmation of the figures: "organizations that sent 2,000 messages had a 93% likelihood of continuing to use Slack long-term," and onboarding (Slackbot prompts to invite teammates, set up channels, integrate tools) was redesigned to reach 2,000 messages faster. — [IdeaPlan, Slack product-led growth case study](https://www.ideaplan.io/case-studies/slack-product-led-growth)

**Superhuman (documented, first-person Vohra)**
- Sean Ellis's PMF benchmark: 40%+ of users answering "very disappointed" if they lost the product. - The engine loop: survey, segment, analyze, implement, track; Vohra focuses on the "very disappointed" segment and "politely disregards" the not-disappointed segment when deciding what to build. — [Mind the Product](https://mindtheproduct.com/the-product-market-fit-engine-by-rahul-vohra); [Business of Software 2018 talk](https://businessofsoftware.org/2018/11/product-market-fit-engine-rahul-vohra-ceo-superhuman/)
- Onboarding was fully manual: a "30-minute one-to-one video call for each new user" (Vohra); earliest sessions were two hours, in person, about five per week; Superhuman had ~20 full-time onboarding staff at peak; the waitlist was ~180,000 (one interview) to 220k+ (another). — [Drift, "Remarkable product onboarding"](https://drift.com/blog/remarkable-product-onboarding/); [Speedrun, "How a former RuneScape designer built a cult hit"](https://speedrun.substack.com/p/how-a-former-runescape-designer-built); [Lenny's Newsletter, Rahul Vohra](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/rahul-vohra); [Mixergy interview](https://mixergy.com/interviews/superhuman-with-rahul-vohra/); [First Round, Superhuman onboarding playbook](https://review.firstround.com/superhuman-onboarding-playbook/)
- An aggregator summary of a Vohra video says the waitlist size "was set by onboarding capacity, not by a desire to look exclusive." (Secondary.) — [echai summary](https://echai.ventures/startingup/r/superhuman-s-waitlist-wasn-t-about-fomo--r-superhuman-s-waitlist-wasn-t-about-fomo)

**Method and caveats (Lenny, Balfour/Reforge, Johns, Mixpanel, Mode, Statsig)**
- Lenny Rachitsky: "a good activation metric is causal for your retention, not just correlative"; method is to "run a regression to find retention inflections, then run some experiments to see if increasing the percentage of users hitting that moment increases their retention rate." — [Lenny's Newsletter, "How to determine your activation metric"](https://lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-determine-your-activation)
- Andy Johns (Facebook, Twitter, Quora, Wealthfront): "Top of the funnel (A) x Magic Moment (B) = Sustainable Growth (C)"; the magic moment is the emotional response of the user when the product delivers core value. — [First Round Review, "Indispensable growth frameworks from my years at Facebook, Twitter and Wealthfront"](https://review.firstround.com/indispensable-growth-frameworks-from-my-years-at-facebook-twitter-and-wealthfront); [Index Ventures, "Growth 101"](https://www.indexventures.com/perspectives/growth-101-wealthfronte28099s-andy-johns-on-how-to-build-and-test-a-sustainable/)
- Brian Balfour / Reforge: activation has three moments, "set up moment, Aha moment, and habit forming moment"; "activation is about establishing the habit for the first time," and onboarding tours do not count; most onboarding designs only for setup and stops too early. (Summaries of Reforge material.) — [Reforge "Activation's Three Moments" summary](https://hq-raydata-co.pages.dev/vault/06-reference/2026-04-03-activations-three-moments/); [echai, Reforge activation summary](https://echai.ventures/startingup/growth-metrics/how-do-i-measure-activation-and-where-should-the-activation-moment-actually-sit-in-my-onboarding)
- Mixpanel: "there really isn't anything inherently magical in a so-called magic number." — [Mixpanel, "Magic numbers are an illusion"](https://mixpanel.com/blog/magic-numbers-are-an-illusion/)
- UX Collective calls these "tipping point metrics" and argues they should be tested rather than dismissed. — [UX Collective, "Magic numbers: the useful illusion"](https://uxdesign.cc/magic-numbers-a-useful-illusion-391baa8751d3)
- Statsig: "Correlation ≈ causation + selection bias"; aha moments reflect user-driven behaviour that depends on hidden factors. — [Statsig, correlation vs causation guide](https://statsig.com/blog/correlation-vs-causation-guide)
- PostHog: the aha moment is rarely a single event; test candidate early actions by measuring retention lift. — [PostHog, activation metrics](https://posthog.com/docs/data-warehouse/pocket-guide/activation-metrics)

### Inferences
- The canonical thresholds share a shape: a small count of network connections (7 friends, 30 follows, 10 follow-backs) or of reciprocal interactions (2,000 team messages) inside a short window (7-10 days). For LaFamilia the natural candidates are "N intro requests sent/accepted in first 14 days" and "first warm reply received," with the reciprocal (follow-back-like) metric probably the causal one.
- With ~250 activated accounts, a formal regression will be noisy; the operator playbook (Superhuman, Johns) points to qualitative segmentation of the 100 opted-in investors and 51 raising founders before any statistics.
- The Slack figure is a team-level metric, not an individual one; the equivalent here is likely a pair or cohort metric (founder + investor who actually exchanged messages) rather than per-account logins.

### Gaps
- Exact Butterfield wording for 2,000 messages/93% could not be verified (First Round text not retrievable); treat as "widely attributed, secondhand-confirmed."
- No primary source for a LinkedIn "connections in first week" threshold or a Dropbox "one file in one folder" threshold; both should be labelled folklore.
- Chamath's verbatim framing of the four-part framework and his causation caveats were not retrievable (talk video is inside a Udemy course).
- Superhuman's later PMF-score improvement (often cited as 58%) was not confirmed in retrievable snippets and is omitted.

## Q3. What does Hoffman say about growing fast while keeping quality (LinkedIn's invite-gated start, "let fires burn", scaling standards)?

### Takeaway
Hoffman's documented account is a deliberately slow, invitation-driven start (13 insiders invited 112 people on day one in May 2003; invite-gated access; ~500,000 members by April 2004), a later contact-import step that "spiked" growth but gave LinkedIn a "spammy" reputation, and the Blitzscaling rule to triage rather than fight every fire ("let fires burn"), with customer service explicitly named as a fire that can wait. No retrievable source has him describing an explicit community-standards curation playbook.

### Cited Findings
- Hoffman: "We started slowly in the first few days because we wanted to make sure the systems worked. I think the 13 people associated with the company invited 112 people." — [CNN Money, June 2 2009](https://money.cnn.com/2009/06/02/smallbusiness/linkedin_startup_story.smb/)
- Public launch date: May 5, 2003. — [The Next Web, LinkedIn at 10](https://thenextweb.com/news/linkedin-10-years-old-milestones)
- Invite-gated early access focused on the founders' Silicon Valley IT contacts; "the invitation-only approach let the team control growth while fixing technical issues." — [Vator, "When LinkedIn was young"](https://vator.tv/2016-08-26-when-linkedin-was-young-the-early-years/)
- Early counts conflict across secondary sources: ~2,000 in the first week (one source), 4,500 members after one month (another), "a mere 245 members by the end of that same year" (a third, almost certainly wrong). — [stratrix](https://www.stratrix.com/vault/linkedin-network-growth); [Startup Savant](https://startupsavant.com/startup-center/linkedin-strategy-story); [Flexiple founder story](https://projects.flexiple.com/startup/founder-stories/reid-hoffman)

- Blitzscaling is reserved for winner-take-most markets with large opportunity and sufficient capital. (Secondary summary.) — [YourStory, Blitzscaling tips](https://yourstory.com/2018/11/blitzscaling-success-tips-hyper-growth-linkedin-reid-hoffman)
- Masters of Scale on early users: scaling ideas "first begin via working closely with a tiny cohort of users" (Airbnb home visits example). — [Masters of Scale book](https://mastersofscale.com/book/)
- Hoffman on network effects as the intended long-term value of LinkedIn. — [NFX interview with James Currier](https://nfx.com/post/reid-hoffman-network-effects-interview-james-currier)
- Later LinkedIn growth leadership expanded metrics to "acquisition, activation, connections, retention, and resurrection." — [Inc., John Koetsier](https://www.inc.com/john-koetsier/the-3-stages-of-linkeds-epic-growth-from-500000-to-500-million-users.html)

### Inferences
- Hoffman's own LinkedIn story is closer to "slow, trusted, invite-gated start, then one well-timed viral mechanic" than to blitzscaling; the blitzscaling advice applies once a winner-take-most dynamic is confirmed, which a curated 1,900-account network has not reached.
- "Let fires burn" cuts the other way for a curated community: the fire that would kill LaFamilia first is loss of investor trust from low-quality intro requests, so that is the one fire not to let burn; slow response times or missing features are the ones to tolerate.

### Gaps
- No retrievable Hoffman source describes how LinkedIn scaled community or quality standards (moderation, vetting) during growth; the request's "scaling community standards" angle is unanswered.
- Stanford CS183C (Blitzscaling course) notes did not surface in any search.
- Whether LinkedIn was strictly invite-only versus open signup with invitation-driven growth is not settled by a primary source; Vator's recollection is the strongest evidence of gating.

## Q4. What did LinkedIn, Facebook and others learn about reactivating dormant signups (personalized hooks, PYMK, notification content)?

### Takeaway
LinkedIn's growth team (Elliot Shmukler, Growth Hackers Conference, ~2013) found "who viewed your profile" emails got a 5% click-through from inactive members versus 20% from active ones, concluded "it's a lot easier to get an active user to do more than to get an inactive user to do anything," and found active members pulling in inactive ones beat marketing-led reactivation. People You May Know began at LinkedIn in 2006 (shown as ads that earned the highest CTR the site had seen) and reached Facebook in May 2008, where it is described as the tool that helped Facebook grow exponentially; Facebook's 2018 notification-spam episode is the documented cautionary tale.

### Cited Findings
- Elliot Shmukler (LinkedIn, joined 2008) spoke at a Growth Hacker conference; LinkedIn's "who looked at your profile" emails drew a 5% click-through rate from inactive users and 20% from active users. — [Developer Nation, "Growth lessons from LinkedIn"](https://developernation.net/blog/growth-lessons-from-linkedin)
- Same talk: "it's a lot easier to get an active user to do more, than it is to get an inactive user to do anything"; the team shifted effort toward engaging active users rather than chasing CTR among inactive ones. — [Developer Nation](https://developernation.net/blog/growth-lessons-from-linkedin)
- Same talk: active users are better at pulling inactive users back than most marketing tactics, provided the product has several ways for them to interact; and inactive users leave for different reasons, so reactivation must be diagnosed by segment rather than treated as one group. — [Developer Nation](https://developernation.net/blog/growth-lessons-from-linkedin)
- Sachin Rekhi's first-person account of LinkedIn growth lessons (worked with Shmukler; LinkedIn grew 20M to 200M+ in that period) exists but was not retrievable. — [Sachin Rekhi, "Growth lessons learned from LinkedIn"](https://sachinrekhi.com/growth-lessons-learned-from-linkedin)
- A later Shmukler interview summary: roughly one-fifth of LinkedIn's growth came through word of mouth; LinkedIn was creating roughly one million duplicate accounts per week at one point. (Secondary summary.) — [Remio summary](https://www.remio.ai/post/growth-hacks-for-startups-from-elliot-shmukler-of-instacart-linkedin-and)
- PYMK: "LinkedIn had launched People You May Know in 2006, originally displaying its suggested connections as ads that got the highest click-through rate the professional networking site had ever seen"; Facebook announced its version in May 2008. — [Gizmodo, "People You May Know: a controversial Facebook feature's 10-year history"](https://gizmodo.com/people-you-may-know-a-controversial-facebook-features-1827981959)
- Marker (excerpt from Steven Levy's *Facebook: The Inside Story*) describes PYMK as the tool that helped Facebook grow exponentially. — [Marker, "The untold history of Facebook's most controversial growth tool"](https://marker.medium.com/the-untold-history-of-facebooks-most-controversial-growth-tool-2ea3bfeaaa66)
- LinkedIn's PYMK was tuned for member value, number of connections formed, and member growth; "well-connected members are more likely to engage and drive viral growth." — [WWW 2015 slides, LinkedIn graph systems](https://www.slideshare.net/SouvikGhosh22/graphexsghosh-53507754)
- LinkedIn (2021) rebalanced PYMK to improve equity for less-connected members and fix the poor experience for very popular members. — [LinkedIn Engineering, "Optimizing PYMK for equity in network creation"](https://engineering.linkedin.com/blog/2021/optimizing-pymk-for-equity-in-network-creation)
- Cautionary case: in 2018 Facebook sent lapsed users notification emails listing unread notifications, messages and invites, and sent notifications to numbers provided for 2FA; CSO Alex Stamos called the 2FA case a "bug." Facebook said the emails were meant to help people reach "notifications from friends they may have missed." — [Boing Boing, July 21 2018](https://boingboing.net/2018/07/21/poisoning-the-well.html); [Gulf Times, "Facebook really wants you back"](https://www.gulf-times.com/story/580370/facebook-really-wants-you-back)
- Facebook deliberately tested turning off email notifications in 2010 to measure their effect. — [Computerworld, 2010](https://www.computerworld.com/article/1518343/facebook-fixes-e-mail-notification-problem.html)
- LinkedIn's "resurrection" was a named stage in its growth metrics. — [Inc., Koetsier](https://www.inc.com/john-koetsier/the-3-stages-of-linkeds-epic-growth-from-500000-to-500-million-users.html)
- Generic practitioner guidance on dormant-list reactivation (scripts) exists from 500 Startups. — [500 Startups, "Before you acquire, reactivate"](https://500.co/content/before-you-acquire-reactivate-how-to-reactivate-your-dormant-email-list-includes-scripts)

### Inferences
- The best-documented reactivation hook is social proof about the dormant person specifically ("X viewed your profile", "you may know Y"), not product news. For LaFamilia the analog is "an investor in your sector viewed your raise" or "3 founders you know joined this month," sent only when true.
- Shmukler's 4x CTR gap implies the 87% dormant accounts should be worked through the 13% active ones (asks to invite, endorse, or intro) rather than through broadcast emails.
- Facebook's 2018 experience shows that notification volume to lapsed users is reputationally expensive; for a trust-based investor network the cost is higher.

### Gaps
- The Shmukler numbers rest on one blog recap; the talk slides/video were not found.
- No Facebook growth-team primary source (e.g., Alex Schultz's lectures) on reactivation content surfaced.
- The original 2006 LinkedIn PYMK data and Facebook's PYMK impact metrics (beyond "grew exponentially") were not retrievable.

## Q5. What do operators recommend for the first ~90 days when supply and demand are both present but not interacting?

### Takeaway
Operators converge on: pick a constrained slice and make it dense (Tavel's "minimum viable happiness", Rachitsky's "win the constrained side first", Graham's deliberately narrow market), then manufacture the first transactions by hand (Graham's "Collison installation", Airbnb's New York trip, AngelList's founders personally emailing deals to ~400 angels they knew and screening both sides before automating), and measure supply-demand conversion, transaction density and repeat rather than account counts (Winters). The closest documented analog to LaFamilia is AngelList in 2010-2011: a hand-curated intro list that produced 12,500 introductions in its first full year.

### Cited Findings
- Casey Winters: build supply and demand together, since scalable supply makes demand acquisition possible while demand gives suppliers a reason to stay; early metrics that matter are "conversion rate between supply and demand, enough transactions that supply sticks around, and enough quality that demand comes back." — [First Round Review, "How to build a scalable marketplace: dos and don'ts"](https://review.firstround.com/how-to-build-a-scalable-marketplace-dos-and-donts-from-the-growth-expert-behind-grubhub-pinterest-and-more/); [Antoine Buteau, lessons from Casey Winters](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-casey-winters/)
- Winters: a local marketplace must create enough supply and demand in each market; company-wide totals conceal markets that do not work. — [First Round podcast, "Building winning marketplaces"](https://review.firstround.com/podcast/building-winning-marketplaces-casey-winters/)
- Reforge/Amplitude on liquidity: with high liquidity "there are high chances of a perfect match between supply and demand for every given buyer and seller. This is why liquidity is such a strong driver of retention"; supply utilization rate (share of available supply used by demand) is a core retention metric. — [Amplitude, "How to evaluate and grow marketplace retention"](https://amplitude.com/blog/how-to-grow-marketplace-retention)
- Lenny Rachitsky's research: "Most successful marketplaces defined and won the constrained side first (usually supply), then let demand follow"; Rover, TaskRabbit and Zillow were the demand-first exceptions. — [Rework summary, supply-first vs demand-first](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/marketplace-growth/supply-first-vs-demand-first); [echai summary of Rachitsky Part 1](https://echai.ventures/startingup/r/how-to-kickstart-and-scale-a-marketplace-business-part-1--r-how-to-kickstart-and-scale-a-marketplace)
- Rachitsky's "28 ways to grow supply in a marketplace" is hosted on Chen's site. — [andrewchen.com, Lenny Rachitsky guest post](https://andrewchen.com/grow-marketplace-supply/)
- Sarah Tavel's Hierarchy of Marketplaces: Level 1 is a "constrained opportunity" where the first thing to solve is user happiness in a small market, not scale; "GMV is a vanity metric. Happy GMV is what matters"; concepts of "happy GMV" and "minimum viable happiness." — [Sarah Tavel, Hierarchy of Marketplaces intro and Level 1](https://sarahtavel.medium.com/the-hierarchy-of-marketplaces-introduction-and-level-1-983995aa218e); [Lenny's Podcast with Tavel, chapter list](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-hierarchy-of-engagement-sarah)
- Paul Graham (July 2013): "one of the most common types of advice we give at Y Combinator is to do things that don't scale." Stripe's "Collison installation": "When anyone agreed to try Stripe they'd say 'Right then, give me your laptop' and set them up on the spot," versus founders who settle for "Great, we'll send you a link." — [Paul Graham, "Do Things That Don't Scale"](https://paulgraham.com/ds.HTML)
- Airbnb: after Graham asked where their market was, the founders flew to New York, stayed in homes rented on Airbnb, had one-on-one conversations with hosts and took better photos of listings; Gebbia said the conversation gave them "permission to do things that don't scale." — [YC blog, "Scaling product at Airbnb with Joe Gebbia and Reid Hoffman"](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/scaling-product-airbnb); [First Round Review, Airbnb design thinking](https://review.firstround.com/how-design-thinking-transformed-airbnb-from-failing-startup-to-billion-dollar-business.md)
- AngelList (2010): "a curated email distribution list of angel and seed stage investors," with Nivi and Naval "screening both the deals sent to the list and the investors allowed to receive them." — [The Next Web, "The quiet rise of AngelList", Oct 4 2010](https://thenextweb.com/news/the-quiet-rise-of-angellist)
- AngelList started by emailing the angels Naval knew; the list grew to about 400 angels; Ravikant: they "always did things manually until they understood how the process worked," then automated. — [Vator, "AngelList pivoted four times to get it right"](https://vator.tv/n/16cd); [Navalmanack, "The story of AngelList"](https://navalmanack.com/secret-sections/the-story-of-angellist)
- AngelList 2011: 500 startups and 2,500 investors joined, 12,500 introductions during the year. — [Vator, Feb 18 2011](https://vator.tv/2019/news/2011-02-18-angellist-sizzles-as-angel-investing-heats-up); [TechCrunch, Sept 23 2013](https://techcrunch.com/2013/09/23/angellist/)
- Superhuman ran every onboarding as a live 30-minute concierge call (earlier: two hours, five a week, in person) and capped intake to onboarding capacity. — [Drift](https://drift.com/blog/remarkable-product-onboarding/); [Speedrun](https://speedrun.substack.com/p/how-a-former-runescape-designer-built)
- Tinder's seed was ~500 attendees at one sponsored USC party; Uber ran per-city launches with autonomous ops teams and Give-$200/Get-$200 driver referrals; Clubhouse gave each member two invites and let engagement earn more. — [andrewchen.com Tinder chapter](https://andrewchen.com/solve-a-hard-problem-cold-start-problem/); [Built In Chen excerpt](https://builtin.com/articles/andrew-chen-cold-start-problem); [Hackernoon on Clubhouse invites](https://hackernoon.com/why-clubhouses-invite-only-strategy-is-still-a-great-growth-hack-jp4733bl)
- Chen: invite-only products tie each new signup to an inviter, and users who arrive with more existing relationships retain better. — [Goodreads highlights of The Cold Start Problem](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/56962799-the-cold-start-problem/10957646-claus-enevoldsen/64c65bf5-2861-42c1-b78e-73f882332649)

### Inferences
- With 100 investors and 51 raising founders already present, LaFamilia is past Chen's "collect the hard side" step and at the "manufacture first transactions" step; the AngelList precedent says the operators themselves should hand-pick and send the first intros (screened on both sides) and only automate once the pattern is clear.
- Winters' and Tavel's metrics translate directly: intro-request acceptance rate (supply-demand conversion), share of opted-in investors who received at least one relevant deal in 30 days (supply utilization), and founder repeat requests (demand quality), measured within one sector/stage slice rather than across the whole base.
- Superhuman's and Tinder's playbooks suggest gating activation to what can be hand-onboarded (e.g., 10-15 founders per week with a live call and a curated investor shortlist), rather than pushing the remaining 87% dormant accounts through self-serve.

### Gaps
- No operator source explicitly addresses the "both sides present but zero interaction" state for a professional/investor network; the AngelList material is the nearest analog and its early intro mechanics (how deals were formatted, how many intros per deal) were not retrievable.
- Casey Winters' GrubHub restaurant-seeding specifics and his "don'ts" list were not retrievable (First Round text blocked).
- No 90-day timeline or benchmark (e.g., target intro-acceptance rates) appears in any retrieved source; such numbers would have to be set internally.
